What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — August 28 – September 2, 1804

The Calumet Bluff Council

Five days, August 28 to September 2, 1804. Where a reservoir lies today, the Corps of Discovery reached a turning point.

On August 28 the keelboat and pirogues came up under a wide bottom at the foot of Calumet Bluff — a chalk-faced rise on the Nebraska side, a mile west of present Aten and, today, the south abutment of Gavins Point Dam itself. The very ground the Corps camped on now anchors the wall that drowned the river. They raised a flagpole and prepared for a council. The name Calumet is the French word for the ceremonial peace pipe, and it was chosen here for exactly that reason: this was to be the meeting place with the Sioux.

It nearly didn’t begin well. That same afternoon, with a hard southwest wind, the larger red pirogue drove onto a snag and was holed — “near Sinking in the opinions of the Crew,” as Clark put it. The men beached it, unloaded it, and patched the hole. Both captains, meanwhile, were sick from “some cause for which we cannot account” — probably the scummed chalk-water of the country they had been drinking. And there was a man unaccounted for: George Shannon, the youngest member of the Corps, had gone ahead with the horses days earlier and inadvertently passed the boats, believing them still ahead of him. Shields and J. Field could not overtake him, so the captains sent John Colter racing upriver with provisions to catch him. As the council ground filled, Shannon was still out there somewhere ahead, alone on the prairie — a loose thread the captains could not yet tie off.

On the afternoon of August 29, Sgt. Pryor and the interpreter Pierre Dorion Sr. arrived on the opposite bank with about seventy Yankton Sioux, including five chiefs. Pryor’s report back is one of the small treasures of the journals — and a genuine first. When he and young Pierre Dorion Jr. had approached the Yankton village up the James River, the men came out to meet them carrying a buffalo robe to bear the visitors into camp on it, an honor reserved for distinguished guests; Dorion had to explain that Pryor was not actually the head of the party. The camp itself was about forty lodges of dressed buffalo skin, painted different colors and, in Clark’s recording of Pryor’s words, “all compact & handsomly arranged… of a Conic form,” each holding ten to fifteen people. This is the first description of a tipi anywhere in the expedition’s records — the conical, painted, buffalo-hide lodge that would become the defining image of the Plains nations. Pryor was the first man of the Corps to stand inside one, and he caught the details: a cedar bush set opposite the door, a buffalo head to one side, a stuffed buffalo skin suspended from the center, lodge poles topped with eagle feathers. The Corps had been among earth-lodge and bark-house peoples until now; here, at the edge of the buffalo country, they met for the first time the architecture of the open grasslands. A fat dog was served as a feast of respect, and Pryor’s men “partook hartily and thought it good & well flavored.”

The formal council ran August 30 and 31, and it is one of the most thoroughly documented stretches of the whole outbound journey — four journals running at once, each catching what the others missed. Under an oak with the flag flying, Lewis delivered the standard message: Jefferson was now their Great Father, the United States wanted peace among the nations and open trade. Then came a decision that landed harder than the captains likely understood. They named the chiefs themselves — pronouncing one man, Weuche (“Shake Hand”), the grand chief and dressing him in a red-laced coat and cocked hat, designating a second chief and three lesser chiefs, and handing each a medal. To Lewis and Clark this was tidy diplomacy: identify a head man, decorate him, deal through him. But Yankton leadership did not work that way — authority was diffuse, earned, and shared among many men, and singling out one “grand chief” by the gift of a coat was a clumsy imposition of a foreign hierarchy. It was a recurring mistake of the captains’ Indian diplomacy, and a quiet source of tension among men who each had standing of their own. The medals and the cocked hat could be worn; the rank behind them could not simply be conferred by strangers from downriver.

The rest of the council was theater and substance in equal measure. There was the air-gun demonstration the captains used as a set-piece at every major council — Lewis’s Girandoni rifle firing ball after ball into a tree to the crowd’s astonishment. After dark came a war dance around a great fire, the musicians beating a deerskin stretched over a half-keg that Lewis had donated, warriors rising one by one to recount their deeds. Clark recorded, with real care, a warrior society — now identified as one of the Plains Akicita societies — whose members took a vow never to retreat and never to take cover in battle, no matter the danger. Only four of an original twenty-two were still living.

On the 31st the Sioux gave their own speeches, and they were frank. The chiefs accepted the captains’ words but pressed their poverty: no powder, no ball, their women and children without clothes. Shake Hand noted, with a dignity that reads as quiet irony two centuries later, that he had taken medals from the Spanish and British before — but those “Great Fathers” had never even given him a cloth to keep the medal off his skin, where the captains had at least dressed him properly. And one of them, the Half Man, delivered a warning the captains would do well to remember: he and Dorion might “open the ears” of the lower bands, but the nations upstream “will not open their ears, and you cannot I fear open them.” It hung in the air — a caution from a man who knew the river ahead far better than the two captains did. The council’s lasting result was political: the captains commissioned Pierre Dorion Sr. as a U.S. agent to stay behind, broker peace among the Sioux and their neighbors, and bring chiefs to Washington the following spring.

The Corps pushed off from Calumet Bluff on September 1, alone again, the river risen three inches overnight. They passed a chalk cliff Clark named “White Bear Cliff,” for a grizzly said to have been killed in a hole in the rock — the first time the great bear of the plains enters the journals, by reputation and by name, though no one in the Corps had yet laid eyes on a living one. The name still marks the spot today. They camped at the lower point of Bon Homme Island. The next day, September 2, a violent northwest wind pinned them down after only four miles, under a 110-foot yellow clay bluff. It was here Clark spent hours on something almost comic in hindsight: surveying, with compass and chain, an “ancient fortification” he was convinced was a vast man-made fortress — describing it in formal military terms, ravelins and sallyports and a glacis and all. It was, in fact, a natural sand ridge. The captains had come up the river primed by the Mound Builder works of the Ohio Valley to see ancient civilizations, and so they saw one. A clean little case of the mind finding the pattern it brought with it.

Then and now. Every campsite named here — Calumet Bluff’s flagpole ground, the ancient-fortification ridge, Bon Homme Island, the camps of August 28 through September 2 — lies under water today, drowned behind Gavins Point Dam. The dam was authorized by the Flood Control Act of 1944 after a devastating 1943 flood, the river was closed in 1955, and the rising water formed the twenty-five-mile-long Lewis and Clark Lake. What the Corps poled and paddled as a wild, braided, island-choked river is now, for that stretch, a reservoir. And there is a second loss harder to photograph: the “endless prairies” the journals describe on every side are largely gone too, broken to farmland generations ago. The Corps moved through a sea of grass under an open sky; the country below the flight path today is a quilt of section lines. The narrow, winding water at either end of the lake is the closest surviving echo of the Missouri they actually knew.
