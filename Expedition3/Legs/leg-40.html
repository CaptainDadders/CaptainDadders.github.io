Five Falls and Eighteen Miles

On June 13, 1805, Lewis walked ahead of the canoes with four men and heard the falls long before he saw them — spray standing over the plain like a column of smoke. By midday he was at the brink of the Great Falls of the Missouri, and the argument that had stalled the Corps at Decision Point was over: the Hidatsa had described these falls, so this was the Missouri. Lewis called it the grandest sight he had ever beheld, then spent most of a page complaining that he had neither the pen nor the pencil to do it justice. Downstream, Clark was fighting canoes up the worst current he had ever seen and dosing Sacagawea, who had fallen seriously ill.

The next morning delivered the bad news. Walking upstream, Lewis found not one fall but five, strung over roughly ten miles of river, the largest of them dropping 87 feet. Nothing could be lined or dragged past that. The same day gave him the closest call of his life so far: having shot a buffalo and neglected to reload, he turned to find a grizzly closing to twenty steps, and with no tree within reach he backed into the river to his waist and presented his espontoon until the bear, for reasons he never understood, wheeled and ran. He wrote the day off as a series of curious adventures.

Great Falls of the Missouri, lithograph by A. E. Mathews from Pencil Sketches of Montana (1868) — sixty-three years after Lewis first saw them, and before any dam. Special Collections, Mansfield Library, University of Montana, Missoula.

The falls took more than boats. Lewis, watching from the bank on June 17, recorded buffalo herds crowded into the river by the sheer weight of their own numbers, swept over the brink and broken on the rocks below — which accounted for the mangled carcasses the party had been passing for days. The trails those herds wore coming down to the water were still cut into the gullies sixty-three years later, when A. E. Mathews crossed below the falls on a log raft to draw them from the left bank. He noted the trails in his caption, and added that since the Helena–Fort Benton road opened, buffalo were becoming scarce in that part of Montana.

Lewis rejoined the main party on the 16th and found Sacagawea barely holding a pulse. He treated her with bark, opium, and mineral water from a sulphur spring he had found nearby, and within days she was out of danger — a recovery that mattered to the whole Corps, since she was their one plausible link to the Shoshone whose horses they would need. Clark meanwhile surveyed the whole run of cataracts and staked out a route on the south side: eighteen and a quarter miles overland. Six men set to building two wagons, wheels sawn from the only sound cottonwood anyone could find and axles cut from the mast of the white pirogue, which was cached and left behind.

One oddity is worth pausing on. On June 20 Clark heard, from the direction of the mountains, a repeated distant booming like a six-pounder firing, on a clear and perfectly still day. He questioned the men, ruled out thunder, and admitted plainly that he could not account for it — noting only that the Hidatsa had said the Rocky Mountains made a great noise, without saying why.

The hauling began on June 21 and did not let up for a week. Cottonwood axletrees and tongues splintered under the loads and were rebuilt in the field with willow. The ground was buffalo-trampled mud baked as hard as frozen earth and studded with prickly pear that drove through doubled moccasin soles. Both captains, writing separately the same evening, described nearly the same scene in nearly the same words: men who fell asleep the instant a halt was called, some limping, some unable to stand — and not one of them complaining. When the wind blew hard enough from behind, they rigged sails on the canoes sitting up on their carriages and got real push out of it, sailing, as more than one journal-keeper noted with delight, across dry land. On the evening of the 25th, after a day that had included yet another grizzly, they got out Cruzatte's fiddle and danced on the grass until ten.

The worst came on June 29. Clark, with York, Charbonneau, and Sacagawea carrying her infant son, sheltered from a sudden storm in a dry ravine near the falls. Within minutes the ravine was a wall of water and mud. Clark shoved Sacagawea up the bank one-handed while gripping his rifle in the other, then hauled up a Charbonneau too frightened to move; the water was at Clark's waist and rising, and behind them the channel ran an estimated fifteen feet deep. It emptied into the river directly above the main cataract. Sacagawea lost the baby's cradleboard and nearly all his clothes, Charbonneau lost his gun and tomahawk, and Clark lost his compass, which was dug out of the mud the next day. Out on the open plain the men hauling baggage caught the same storm as hail, bare-headed, and were knocked flat again and again. Clark issued the whole shaken party a dram of grog that night.

Clark's men made the last haul on July 1 and reached the upper camp at White Bear Islands at three in the afternoon with the final canoe. For the first time in more than two weeks the Corps was whole again, above the falls and back on the water. Eighteen miles had cost them the better part of a month. What waited was the problem of boats — and in the morning, Lewis meant to start assembling the iron-frame Experiment he had carried in pieces all the way from Harpers Ferry for exactly this moment.
