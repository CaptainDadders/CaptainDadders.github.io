Today's flight took me over Fort Peck Lake immediately after takeoff. This lake was created by the Fort Peck Dam, completed in 1940. The lake is the largest one in Montana, by surface area, and it extends around 134 miles in length. It submerges around 12 of the campsites from the Lewis and Clark Expedition.

Fort Peck Lake (formerly the Missouri River)

The land around the lake is mostly empty of development. I did not pass a single town while flying over it. And, I had to make a relatively long flight today because there are essentially no airstrips along the reservoir, once past Fort Peck Airstrip.

The closest thing to civilization along the lake that I saw was the James Kipp Recreation Area, near the US 191 bridge.

James Kipp Recreation Area on the left, US 191 bridge ahead.

I also passed the UL Bend. Lewis and Clark wrote about the abundant wildlife in this area, as well as the ruggedness of the countryside.

What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — May 10 – 24, 1805

Fifteen days of wind, bears, and one bad squall

Every camp the Corps made between May 10 and May 20, 1805 now lies under Fort Peck Lake. The stretch was the slowest of their spring. On the 10th they made four and a quarter miles before the wind pinned them to a bend and held them there all day. A stray dog swam over from the far shore, which was enough to convince the captains the Assiniboine were still somewhere ahead: the arms of the whole party were inspected and hunters were sent onto the hills less for meat than to find people. They found nothing. Lewis spent the idle hours measuring a buck's ears — eleven inches — and arguing the French engagés out of “black-tailed deer” in favor of a name of his own. Mule deer stuck. Boils and sore eyes were running through the party; Bratton could not use one hand.

Bratton walked ashore anyway the next day, shot a grizzly through both lungs, and was run half a mile by it. Eight men followed the blood a mile through rose brush and found the animal still alive two hours later, lying in a bed two feet deep it had dug for itself. Lewis: “these bear being so hard to die reather intimedates us all; I must confess that I do not like the gentlemen and had reather fight two Indians than one bear.” A day later he quietly rewrote his own policy, resolving to act on the defensive only when walking alone. On the 14th six of the best hunters in the party tested the math. They crept to within forty paces of a grizzly lying in the open, put four balls into it — two of them through both lobes of the lungs — and were routed. Two men threw away their rifles and jumped off a twenty-foot bank into the river; the bear went in a few feet behind the second. It took eight balls in all, the last through the head.

Hunters in flight from the wounded grizzly, May 14, 1805. Courtesy U.S. National Park Service, Jefferson National Expansion Memorial.

That same afternoon both captains were ashore on the far bank, three hundred yards off, when a squall caught the white pirogue under sail. Charbonneau was at the helm in Drouillard's place, could not swim, and luffed her up into the wind instead of running before it. She went over on her side and filled to within an inch of the gunwales. Aboard were the instruments, the papers, the books, the medicine and most of the trade goods. Lewis dropped his gun and had his coat half unbuttoned to swim for her before he recognized the folly of it. Cruzatte saved her — two men bailing with kettles that happened to be at hand, two more rowing — after the bowsman threatened to shoot Charbonneau where he sat if he did not take hold of the rudder. Sacagawea, in the stern, caught the articles floating away.

Two days went to drying cargo on the bank under a cloudy sky. The medicine took the worst of it; some garden seeds, a little powder and a few kitchen articles went to the bottom. Counting what had survived on the 16th, Lewis wrote that he ascribed to Sacagawea “equal fortitude and resolution with any person onboard at the time of the accedent.” They named the place Panther camp, not for the bear or the pirogue but for a cougar caught caching a half-eaten deer. A white bear shredded Labiche's coat, left out on the plains — by then the grizzlies were a nuisance rather than a terror. Lewis had also begun setting elk hides aside for the leather boat he meant to build at the falls.

From there the men walked the bank with a rope over the shoulder for most of a week. Clark nearly trod on a rattlesnake of a kind neither captain had seen, which Lewis measured and described down to the 176 scales on its belly; that night a burning tree leaned over the lodge and dropped its top exactly where the lodge had stood minutes before, and Lewis reckoned they would have been crushed to atoms. On the 18th came the first rain deserving the name since Fort Mandan, and the Missouri began running clear over gravel instead of mud. On the 19th fog held them until eight. A grizzly shot through the heart ran a quarter mile before dropping. Seaman was bitten through the hind leg by a beaver and Lewis, struggling to stop the bleeding, feared for the dog. From the highest hill he could find, Clark saw the mouth of the Musselshell and, forty or fifty miles west, a range of mountains — the first of the journey.

They reached the Musselshell on the 20th, measured it at 110 yards, and stopped for the day. Three things got named: Blowing Fly Creek, for the insects in the roasting meat; the Musselshell itself, confirming what the Hidatsa had told them at Fort Mandan; and, five miles up, “Sâh-câ-gar me-âh or bird woman's River, after our interpreter the Snake woman.” Far behind them that same day, the keelboat sent down from Fort Mandan in April tied up at St. Louis. Then the wind returned. A night on the sandbars of the great bend filled the air with so much dust that they could neither cook, eat, nor sleep, and they moved the lodge to the foot of a hill in the dark. Particles of snow fell on the 22nd.

The 23rd opened with water frozen on the oars and strawberries in bloom, and closed with mosquitoes. Lewis, trying to understand a country whose creeks ran salt, drank some by way of experiment and reported it “moderately pergative, but painfull to the intestens in it's opperation” — then found a town of burrowing squirrels that appeared to solve the problem by never drinking at all. On the 24th a southeast breeze finally let them raise sail after a week on the rope, and they made twenty-five miles into the Upper Missouri Breaks, camping a few miles above the crossing where U.S. 191 spans the river today. Game was thinning as the timber did. “The beaver appears to keep pace with the timber,” Lewis wrote, “as it declines in quantity they also become more scarce.”
