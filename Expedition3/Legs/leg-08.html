Took off from Lewis Field on a calm evening. Enjoyed watching the sun descending over Ellis Island and the French Islands in the Ohio River. Apparently, these are popular spots for boaters to hang out.

From left to right: Ellis Island, French Island Number 1, French Island Number 2

I passed 2 towns of notable size: Owensboro, Kentucky, and Evansville, Indiana. Neither of these were towns in 1803 when Lewis & Clark sailed past. Owensboro was settled in the 1790s as Yellow Banks, KY, but there would have only been a few people living along the river banks. Evansville was not founded until 1812.

Evansville, Indiana from low above the river

Landed in Henderson, KY, just a few miles south of Evansville. Landing was ok. Floated a bit and a couple of little bounces, but kept the plane pointed down the runway.

What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — Oct 30 – Nov 2, 1803

A River Town Already Waiting

By late October 1803, the Corps of Discovery was making good time down the Ohio, eager to push westward before winter fully closed in. This leg's stretch of river — from the area of present-day Lewisport downstream past Owensboro and on to Henderson — was among the quietest and most rural sections they had yet encountered. The future sites of Owensboro and Evansville were barely settled, if at all. Yellow Banks, as Owensboro was known, had only a handful of scattered settlers along its clay-yellow riverbanks. Evansville did not yet exist.

Henderson was a different story. Unlike those two cities, Henderson was already a real, if young, town when the Corps floated past. It had been formally laid out in April of 1797 by General Samuel Hopkins, acting for the Transylvania Company, and named for Colonel Richard Henderson — the same land speculator who had once commissioned Daniel Boone to blaze the Wilderness Road. At six years old in 1803, Henderson was one of the more established outposts on this stretch of the Ohio, though still a modest riverfront settlement compared to what it would become.

The journals from this stretch are brief — the river was straightforward and the weather was holding. The Corps pressed on, logging miles before the bend to the Mississippi still hundreds of miles ahead. The great adventure of the Missouri River lay beyond that. For now, the Ohio rolled westward beneath autumn skies, and the crew of the keelboat rowed and poled through a wilderness that would look almost unrecognizable within a generation.
