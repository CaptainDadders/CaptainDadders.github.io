What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — Jul 18 – 23, 1805

Rocks Ready to Tumble

The captains needed horses. The Missouri was running out of navigable water, the mountains ahead were unknown, and the only people who could sell them pack animals or describe what lay beyond were the Shoshone — who had not been seen, only inferred. So on July 18 Clark went overland with Joseph Field, John Potts and York to find them, getting out ahead of the party's hunting rifles, since a stranger's gunfire in that country would send the Shoshone into the mountains rather than toward the river. He found their road the same evening: wide, and dug out in places by hand. Until then the Corps had been guessing whether anyone lived in these mountains at all. Now the only question was where they were at that moment.

Late the next evening the canoes entered a gorge where the cliffs rose straight out of the water — Lewis put them at 1,200 feet — and the river had worn a channel barely wider than itself. For three miles there was no spot where a man could have set his foot down. The rocks seemed ready to tumble on the boats; there was nowhere to stand to use a cord or a pole, so it was oars or nothing, and full dark before Lewis found enough flat ground to sleep on. He named it himself: the gates of the rocky mountains. That same night, on the high ground, Clark pulled seventeen prickly pear spines out of his feet by firelight.

Past the gorge the country opened. On the 20th smoke rose seven miles up a valley, as if the plain had been fired — they had been seen, and Clark, guessing the party had been taken for a war party, began leaving signs along his trail to say white men, friends. His feet gave out the next day and he stopped to hunt and wait. Then on the 22nd Sacagawea looked at the river and said her people lived on it, and that the Three Forks were close — the place where three rivers meet to make the Missouri, the end of the river the Corps had been climbing since St. Louis and the doorway to whatever came after it. She had been a girl here when a Hidatsa raiding party carried her east; this was the reason she was on the expedition at all, and the first proof it would pay. The Shoshone were no longer a hopeful rumor — they were upriver, with horses. Lewis wrote that it cheered the spirits of the whole party. On the 23rd he ordered every canoe to fly its small flag, so that anyone watching from the hills could see the men coming up were not Indians and not enemies.
