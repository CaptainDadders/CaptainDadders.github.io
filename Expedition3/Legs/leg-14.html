After wintering at Camp Dubois and Cahokia, just across the river from St. Louis, William Clark and the crew finally started up the Missouri River on May 14, 1804. It was Spring, and the Louisiana Territory was now officially part of the United States. Captain Lewis stayed behind; he was organizing a trip for Native American leaders to head to Washington to meet President Jefferson, "their new father". Clark headed up the Missouri to St. Charles, Missouri, the last major European settlement for the remainder of the journey.

The Corps waited in St. Charles for Captain Lewis to arrive. Lewis arrived by horseback on May 20. They stayed a couple more days before heading out into the wilderness.

The Point of Departure

After a winter of drilling, recruiting, and waiting out the formal handover of Upper Louisiana, the Corps of Discovery finally pushed off. On the afternoon of May 14, 1804, William Clark and roughly forty men shoved the keelboat and two pirogues into the current at the mouth of the Wood River, fired the swivel gun, and started up the Missouri. Meriwether Lewis was not aboard — he had stayed in St. Louis to close out the expedition’s business, including arranging for a delegation of Osage chiefs to travel east under Pierre Chouteau and meet President Jefferson in Washington.

Clark led the boats the short distance up to St. Charles, the French-Canadian town the locals called “Petite Côte,” reaching it on May 16. With about 450 inhabitants, it was the last established town the Corps would see for more than two years. They lingered several days, taking on a handful of experienced French boatmen — among them Pierre Cruzatte and François Labiche — and shifting cargo to better balance the heavily loaded vessels, while the townspeople welcomed the men to dances, dinners, and Sunday church.

Lewis rode overland from St. Louis, about twenty-five miles, and rejoined the party on the evening of May 20. The next afternoon, May 21, the captains set out from St. Charles together for the first time. A stiff headwind and heavy rain held them to barely three miles before they camped on the north bank, with storms rolling through the night — a fittingly stubborn welcome to the river that would test them all the way to the mountains.

The true edge of the settled world still lay a few days ahead. On May 25 the expedition passed La Charrette, a cluster of seven poor French houses near present-day Marthasville — what the journals call the last settlement of whites on the Missouri. Beyond it the maps gave out. Lewis would later designate the mouth of the Wood River as the expedition’s official “point of departure,” but it was here, leaving St. Charles and then La Charrette behind, that the Corps finally rowed off the edge of the known.
