Adieu to the Experiment

On July 2, 1805, the last kegs came in from the six-mile stake and the Great Falls portage was finally over. The camp at the White Bear Islands turned at once to Lewis's favorite project: the collapsible iron boat frame he had carried from the Harpers Ferry armory. He planned to skin it in elk and buffalo hide and load it with the heaviest cargo. The frame went together in about three hours. After that, almost everything went wrong. There was no pine in the country, so the tar kiln yielded nothing. The needles had sharp edges that cut the hides, and as the skins dried the stitch holes gaped open. Lewis's only substitute sealant was pounded charcoal mixed with beeswax and buffalo tallow. He admitted in his journal that he feared it would not work.

On July 4 the men drank the last of the spirits and danced to the fiddle until a thunderstorm drove them under cover. The captains had quietly dropped their plan to send a canoe back downriver with dispatches, because they could not yet spare a single man. Lewis wrote that the hardest part of the voyage lay ahead, “yet I see no one repining.”

On July 8 the boat was paid with the composition, and the men named her the Experiment. The next day she was launched and “lay like a perfect cork on the water.” Then a violent wind forced the canoes to be unloaded, and by the time it dropped, most of the sealant had peeled from the seams and she leaked beyond saving. Lewis worked out the cause that same evening. The one section left with a little hair on the hide had stayed dry, so singeing the skins instead of shaving them would have saved her. But the buffalo were leaving and the season was advancing, and further experiments “seemed to me madness.” He wrote, “I bid a dieu to my boat, and her expected services.” Burying the frame the next morning took two hours.

Full-size replica of the Experiment’s iron frame at the Lewis & Clark National Historic Trail Interpretive Center, Great Falls. Photo by Barry Swackhamer, HMdb.org

The replacement came out of a cottonwood bottom near present-day Ulm. It was six miles away by land and more than twenty by water. Clark's party found only a couple of trees big enough, both windshaken or hollow. The choppers broke thirteen chokecherry axe handles in part of a single day. Lewis wrote, “I feel excessively anxious to be moving on.” On July 13 he walked overland to the canoe camp with Sacagawea and a sick man. On the way they passed the frame of an enormous lodge, 216 feet around, whose purpose Lewis admitted he could not guess. The two new dugouts, 25 and 33 feet long, were launched the next evening.

At 10 A.M. on July 15, eight heavily laden canoes pushed off. Lewis wrote, “we once more saw ourselves fairly under way much to my joy.” They passed the mouth of a river they named for Navy Secretary Robert Smith, and the flat-topped butte they called Fort Mountain. On the 16th the party found about forty deserted willow shelters and horse tracks. It was the first fresh sign of the Shoshone, and Lewis took it as real hope. Going ahead, he reached the spot where the Missouri leaves the plains. There he named a 400-foot rock standing in the gap “the tower” and climbed it for a last look at the country behind them. The next day the Corps entered the canyon, lining the canoes up a rapid and crossing and recrossing the river beneath sheer rock walls. They saw bighorn sheep on the summits above. They camped for the night of July 17 near where I-15 now crosses the Missouri.
