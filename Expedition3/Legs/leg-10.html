After landing in Metropolis earlier this evening, I had time to make the short hop over to Cairo. Not long after takeoff, I reached Cairo, IL, and the end of the Ohio River.

The end of the Ohio River. Cairo, IL to the right, and the Mississippi River

When I think of Cairo, I think of Mark Twain's Huckleberry Finn. Huck and Jim travel down the Mississippi in a raft, intending to flee from the slave state of Missouri, stop at Cairo, and catch a steamboat up the Ohio River to a free state. Unfortunately, they miss Cairo in a foggy night, and end up further down the Mississippi, deeper into slave territory.

Mississippi River on the left, Cairo Regional Airport on the right

It was also, of course, an important waypoint on the Lewis & Clark journey. Their trip thus far had been all down-river. Once on the Mississippi, they had to go upstream, against the river flow, making travel more difficult. They are still in familiar territory, but they are reaching St. Louis and the Missouri River, where the real exploration begins.

My landing was somewhat dramatic. I was facing a strong headwind and coming in too slow. I had a bit of a crosswind, but as I slowed, my rudder was losing effectiveness and I ended up drifting a little off the runway. Since I was going too slow, I was unable to compensate in time, and ended up stalling and bouncing, and nearly clipped my wing on the runway. It was not pretty, but I kept everything in one piece, so I can continue up the Mississippi toward St. Louis.

What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — Nov 13–14, 1803

Turning North on the Mississippi

Two days after leaving Fort Massac, the Corps of Discovery reached the confluence of the Ohio and the Mississippi — the same waters visible from Cairo today. Lewis and Clark recorded their arrival on November 14, 1803, and would remain camped at the junction for nearly a week, using the days to take the celestial observations needed to fix their position on the map.

The stop also marked the end of easy progress. Every mile since Pittsburgh had been downstream — the Ohio carried the keelboat and pirogues with the current, day after day. Now, on the Mississippi, the Corps faced a far more powerful river running the wrong direction. Going upstream toward St. Louis would mean fighting the current for weeks. But it was still familiar territory, still within the bounds of a country that had mapped and settled this corridor for generations. Beyond St. Louis, where the Missouri River joined from the west, the true wilderness began — and with it, the real purpose of the expedition.
