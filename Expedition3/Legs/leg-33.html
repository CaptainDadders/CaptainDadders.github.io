Back in the air today after a layover of more than a month. This was a fitting place to take a break, because on this leg, I flew over the site where Lewis and Clark settled down for the winter of 1804-1805. They built a camp near the Mandan and Knife River Villages, a prominent trading area for the Native Americans. It is here where Lewis and Clark met Sacagawea, then only 16 years of age, and her 46-year-old husband, Toussaint Charbonneau. The expedition hired both of them for the remainder of the journey, to serve as interpreters.

After a month out of the cockpit, I was a bit worried that I would have forgotten how to fly this plane, and especially how to land it, but the flight went just fine.

The Knife River flows into the Missouri

Around half-way through the flight, I passed the Knife River, near which Lewis and Clark spent the winter. Their actual winter home was Fort Mandan, southeast of the Knife River and now completely submerged under water, because, well, dams. If you were to find the spot on the river where the fort was built, you would have a view of Leland Olds Station, a 660 MW coal power plant built in 1966.

Five Villages and a Long Winter

The last week of October brought the Corps its first snow, and with it the unmistakable sense that a season, not just a river, was closing in. Above the Heart River the party passed corridor after corridor of collapsed earth lodges — nine mounds in twenty miles by Clark's count, human skulls scattered among the fallen timbers. These were the old Mandan towns, a once-powerful nation of nine villages that smallpox had gutted in 1781 and Sioux raiders had finished off afterward, driving the survivors fifty miles upriver to a last cluster of five villages at the mouth of the Knife River. The captains were paddling into a graveyard before they ever met a living Mandan. Along the way the Arikara chief traveling with them, Too Né, pointed out a sacred medicine stone said to foretell the future and an old lightning-scarred oak where young men had once pierced their own flesh in a version of the Sun Dance — a guided tour of Mandan sacred geography delivered before the Corps had exchanged a word with the people themselves.

They reached the first lodge fire on October 26, at Mitutanka, the lower of two Mandan villages clustered with three allied Hidatsa towns in a five-village complex that made this stretch of the Missouri the great trading crossroads of the northern Plains. Two Mandan chiefs came aboard — one an Arikara by birth, the other an adopted Cheyenne, a detail that told the captains something true about how loosely national identity worked here. An Irish trader named Hugh McCracken, already resident with goods to trade, was proof the British had been doing business with these villages for a generation before any American arrived. The chiefs were delighted by Lewis's hand-cranked steel corn mill, and equally delighted by the sight of York; Clark, walking to the second village, found a nation he judged uncommonly friendly, even as he and Lewis quietly agreed never to leave the boat unattended together until they better understood their hosts. Two days later, moving four miles further, the Corps raised a flagstaff opposite the Knife River villages and invited all five towns to council.

Wind wrecked the first attempt. The council finally convened on October 29 in a tent walled with ship's sails against blowing sand, the familiar Jeffersonian speech delivered once more — a new "Great Father" in Washington, peace among nations, trade to come. At its center was the errand the captains had carried for weeks: persuading the Mandans and Hidatsas to smoke a peace pipe with Too Né's Arikara. They did, "with eagerness." Medals went out in three graded ranks; the captains met Sheheke, called Big White, chief of the lower village, and Black Cat, the Mandans' grand chief at the upper village, the two men who would anchor the winter to come. Two days later Black Cat answered formally, in his own lodge, wrapping a decorated buffalo robe around Clark's shoulders and sending a delegation of his own people south with Too Né to carry the peace to the Arikara villages in person — as concrete a diplomatic result as the expedition would ever produce. That same week a runaway prairie fire killed two Mandans and nearly a third, a boy saved only because his mother threw a green buffalo hide over him — an accident the Mandans read as evidence he carried "Great Spirit medicine," and Clark, characteristically, read as a fireproof hide. With the river dropping and ice liable to close it at any time, the captains decided to winter here.

Fort Mandan rose through November in a cottonwood grove a few miles below the villages, named, in Lewis's words, "in honour of our Neighbours." Around its construction the party's winter cast assembled: René Jusseaume, a Mandan interpreter the captains distrusted on sight and kept anyway; and, on November 4, Toussaint Charbonneau, a French-Canadian trader who arrived with two young Shoshone wives among his household and was hired to interpret. One of those wives began appearing in the journals that same week, unnamed, bringing buffalo robes to the fort — the woman history would come to know as Sacagawea, though the record would not name her outright for months yet, letting her come into focus gradually rather than all at once. Through November and December the fort filled in around them: an aurora display recorded for its beauty, hunters gone so long the camp grew anxious before they returned with two thousand pounds of meat, ice running in the river, frostbite becoming a recurring winter injury even as the men moved into finished huts by month's end. A Sioux and Arikara raiding party killed a Mandan hunter within sight of the fort on November 30; Clark rode out with an armed party intending pursuit before the Mandans talked him into waiting for spring, a small lesson in how thin the captains' promised protection actually ran.

Cold settled in hard through December, deep enough that Sergeant Gass could freeze proof spirits solid in fifteen minutes, and a barter economy took shape that would carry the fort through the whole winter: Mandan and Hidatsa corn, squash, and buffalo meat flowing in, blacksmith work — mended hoes, axes, bridles, and eventually forged war axes — flowing back out. It was arguably the single most valuable thing the Corps had to offer its hosts. Christmas brought cannon fire, brandy, and fiddles playing until nine at night, with no Mandan visitors allowed that one day by the captains' own request. Through January the men danced at the villages at the Mandans' own invitation — York among them, to general astonishment at how lightly so large a man could move — while North West Company traders came and went with British medals and flags the captains worked, not always successfully, to keep out of Mandan hands. On February 11, after a long and difficult labor eased along by a folk remedy of crushed rattlesnake rattle, Sacagawea gave birth to a son, Jean Baptiste, at the fort — the point at which the journals finally describe her plainly enough to use her name without guessing. Days later a Sioux raid on the hunting camp sent Lewis out with more than twenty volunteers in a week-long pursuit that covered ground but never caught the raiders. By the end of February the fort turned its labor toward six dugout canoes for the spring, cut and hollowed from cottonwood over the following weeks, while the visiting Le Borgne, the Hidatsa's own guarded and skeptical grand chief, paid his one call in March, more impressed by the blacksmith shop than by anything else the Americans had brought. Charbonneau, accused by the captains in mid-March of having been worked on by rival traders, quit under an ultimatum over his conditions of service — and, before the ice broke, thought better of it and came back.

By April 7 the canoes were finished and the fort's long pause ended. In a single afternoon the expedition split in two: the keelboat turned downstream for St. Louis under a discharged corporal, carrying maps, reports, and crates of specimens meant for President Jefferson, while the permanent party — captains, sergeants, privates, Charbonneau, Sacagawea and infant Jean Baptiste, and a Mandan man willing to travel as far as the Shoshone — pushed upriver in two pirogues and six canoes into country no American had yet mapped. Lewis, watching his "little fleet" pull away, called the moment among the happiest of his life. The next morning he walked ahead on shore to take a final leave of Black Cat, smoking a parting pipe and receiving a pair of moccasins from the chief who had been the Corps' steadiest Mandan friend all winter, while behind him a swamped canoe cost the party half a bag of biscuit and a serious share of gunpowder — a small, immediate reminder that the sheltered months at the fort were over and the river's ordinary hazards had resumed.
