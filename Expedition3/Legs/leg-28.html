When I have the chance to fly during the day, I like to fly with realtime weather. Sometimes this is sketchy. Today was such a day. At my destination airport, 9V9, Chamberlain Municipal Airport, METAR showed winds 19-24 kt from 160° - 170°. That's a pretty stiff breeze, especially for a small plane. Fortunately, I had good choices for runways, either 13 or 18, which should make this mostly a headwind, which is what you want for landing. Crosswinds are bad. Taking off from Pepper Port, where I had a pretty adventurous landing yesterday, I just had to hope that the winds wouldn't be too horrible.

Flying over Lake Francis Case, it did still look like a river, just a very fat one. The shape of the river is generally intact -- it's as if someone took a fat highlighter and traced over a fine-point pencil sketch. The details of the sketch are lost, like the river bottom that has been drowned. This lake is 107 miles long -- miles that used to be a wild river.

Lake Francis Case, looking like a fat river

But I didn't have much time to worry about that. As I flew, I kept checking the wind. I changed my landing choice from Runway 18 to Runway 13 based on the current wind readings. I made a right turn from the river/lake, and saw Interstate 90 of my left wing. The bridge over the river is called the Lewis and Clark Memorial Bridge. I have driven over that bridge, years ago. As I continued on final, the plane was getting buffeted quite a bit, and I knew it was going to be... interesting. At one point I checked the instruments and it showed a 20kt headwind and an 10kt crosswind. Yikes. Not ideal. I put more and more rudder into the wind, and had to give extra throttle to keep from stalling with the big headwind. The plane seemed to float, almost hover, over the runway, before finally touching down on the left side of the pavement. But I quickly came to a stop on account of the fact that I was barely moving when I landed. I took a breath, and without the parking brake on, the plane actually started rolling backwards. I quickly and carefully taxied off the runway -- crosswinds can tip a little plane like this over even after you've already landed.

What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — Sep 10 – 17, 1804

Starved in a Land of Plenty

By the second week of September 1804, the Corps of Discovery was deep in what is now south-central South Dakota, working the keelboat and two pirogues up a Missouri that the Norden crossed two centuries later as a single broad sheet of impounded water. The men of 1804 saw none of that stillness. They saw a braided, sandbar-choked river and, spreading away from both banks, an abundance the like of which no one in their party had words for — herds beyond counting. On September 10 the Corps camped on the Mud Islands, and on a hilltop nearby they found a fossil backbone lying connected for some forty-five feet, with petrified teeth and ribs. They had no concept of what it was; the word “dinosaur” would not be coined for nearly forty years. They took it for the bones of a monstrous large fish, packed some of the vertebrae, and shipped them east. It was, in fact, a plesiosaur — a creature of an ancient inland sea, beached now on the top of a prairie bluff.

Then, on September 11, the open thread of the previous leg finally closed. George Shannon — at eighteen the youngest man in the Corps — had vanished on August 26 while chasing stray horses, and the party had been looking for him behind them ever since. He had been ahead of them the whole time, walking up the bank in the belief that the boat was somewhere upriver. Around one in the afternoon he was spotted coming down toward the river on horseback, gaunt and spent. He had been gone the better part of three weeks, and for most of that time he had nothing to eat but wild grapes and a single rabbit, which he killed by jamming a piece of hard stick into his rifle for want of a bullet. Buffalo had wandered within thirty yards of his camp, and he could not kill one. Clark set it down in a line that has outlived almost everything else in the day’s entry: a man had nearly starved to death in a land of plenty for want of something to kill his meat. Shannon, too weak to go on, had decided to lay by and wait for a trader’s boat he hoped was coming up behind — keeping one horse as a last resort, prepared to eat it if no one came.

The same stretch of river produced one of the most cinematic single passages in the whole expedition, and it belongs to Sergeant John Ordway, usually the party’s driest bookkeeper of distances and meat tallies. Walking the south shore in the rain on September 11, he climbed a red hill and found sixteen bull buffalo feeding below. He crept close for a side shot. One bull caught sight of him — Ordway was wearing a red shirt, army issue, common enough among the men — and instead of bolting, the animal stared, then walked straight up to him. With no other option Ordway fired at its head; the ball lodged in the long hair and the bull ran off, and the rain promptly fouled his gun as he reloaded. It is the scene the painter Michael Haynes set on canvas as Red Shirt: a lone man in the rain, a buffalo bull at arm’s length, the rest of the herd grazing as if nothing had happened.

Red Shirt — Michael Haynes, www.mhaynesart.com. Sgt. Ordway’s September 11 encounter with a buffalo bull.

The country itself was the other great character of these days. On September 12 the keelboat met its match at an island the men named, with feeling, Troublesome Island: the channel was so crowded with sand that the boat turned on the bars four times and nearly capsized, the crew leaping into the river to hold her upright. It took three-quarters of a day to make a single mile. Recovery came slowly. Through it all the naturalists kept working — Lewis killing and carefully describing a porcupine and a blue-winged teal, the herbarium growing by blazing star and goldenrod and milkvetch, the prairie-dog towns rolling past one after another, some hundreds of yards across.

September 14 brought two landmark firsts. Clark shot a male pronghorn — the first taken and first scientifically described by the expedition — and Lewis recorded its weight and measurements and its strange hooked horn, noting that the animal seemed more like the antelope of Africa than any goat. The same day Shields killed a white-tailed jackrabbit, also new to science, nearly white and bigger than any hare known in the States. Both skins were stuffed for shipment back to Washington. The Corps named the creek they camped on Shannon’s Creek, for the man who had survived on its grapes; it stands today, renamed, as Bull Creek. Two miles on, near present Oacoma, they reached the mouth of the White River, and Clark looked at the bend and judged it a beautiful situation for a town. He was right: Chamberlain and Oacoma sit there now, where the Norden came down in a hard south wind, in sight of the bridge that carries Lewis and Clark’s names across the drowned river.

Head of an Antelope (pronghorn) — Karl Bodmer, 1832–34, painted about 28 years after the expedition. Joslyn Art Museum.

Above Corvus Creek — named when Lewis killed a magpie there, a bird unknown in the eastern states — the captains called a halt at a timbered, plum-rich bottom they named Pleasant Camp, to dry their rain-soaked cargo and refit. Here they made one of the quiet, consequential decisions of the whole year: rather than send a return party back down to St. Louis from this point with the journals and specimens, they would keep everyone and everything together and continue north, holding the keelboat and its records for the winter quarters still ahead. The day after settling in, September 17, Lewis took six hunters out onto the high plain and looked west over a scene he could hardly bring himself to report without disclaiming exaggeration: buffalo, elk, deer, and antelope feeding in every direction, perhaps three thousand bison in a single view. It is the image these September days leave behind — a land so full of game that a man could still, somehow, starve in the middle of it.
