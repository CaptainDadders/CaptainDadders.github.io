Another evening flight, and I got to watch the North Dakota sunset. I continued up the Missouri River and reached the confluence of the Cannonball River, so named because of the large and round stones in the river that resembled cannonballs. In fact, Lewis and Clark took one of these stones on board to use as an anchor.

Cannonball River, to the left

I flew past the outskirts of Bismarck, ND, before turning left away from the river toward Mandan Municipal Airport (Y19), just south of Mandan, ND. The Mandan Villages played a significant part in the Lewis and Clark journey. These villages were a trading hub, and it is where they ended up spending the winter of 1804-1805. However, that was a bit further up the river from where I landed.

Landed on grass at Mandan, and tried out the technique of dumping the flaps on touchdown, and more of a 2-wheel landing. Less bouncy than normal, but also a bit less stable; had to make a correction to keep the plane from tipping over. Yet another learning experience.

The Cannonball and the Slant Village

October 15 was the last day of any meaningful Arikara contact on the outbound trip. The boat covered just ten miles and passed three hunting camps in that distance — the first some thirty men, women, and children crossing in twelve buffalo-skin bull boats laden with meat and skins, the captains halting two hours to eat with them and trade fishhooks for cooked meat. The river was nothing like empty country; it was a fall-hunt thoroughfare. York remained the focus he had been since the Arikara villages: Ordway records that the children would follow after him, and if he turned toward them they would run shouting as if terrified. By Whitehouse’s note, this was the last Arikara hunting camp they expected to see.

The next morning the keelboat pushed upstream with the Arikara chief Too Né — Eagle Feather, also called Arketarnarshar — still aboard, traveling with the Corps to council with the Mandans. Two young Arikara women who had followed the boat from the villages were anxious to come along, and the captains were now in country the chief knew intimately. Above camp they passed an old earthen-walled circular fort — the Cheyenne’s “first stand,” Clark wrote, after the Sioux had driven them west from the head of the Red River of Lake Winnipeg. The chief named the streams as they passed — Girl Creek, Woman Creek, place of Beaver, the river called “Elk shed their horns” — and Clark wrote each one down.

Late in the day Clark saw what he had no frame for. Pronghorn by the score were in the river, swimming for the western shore, and Arikara boys had pushed in among them with sticks and were clubbing them in the water. Men on both banks shot them with bow and arrow and turned back any that tried to climb out; mounted hunters worked the flanks to keep the herd in the channel “So that they might kill as many as they pleased.” Clark counted fifty-eight killed from one swimming gang. The Corps’ own hunters added three more. The next morning, walking ashore with Too Né and the interpreter Joseph Gravelines, Clark got the framing for what he had watched: the pronghorn were not local. They were moving south and west each fall, swimming the Missouri to winter in the Black Hills timber and dispersing back to the plains in spring. Lewis put it plainly in his weather diary: “Antilopes are passing to the black hills to winter, as is their custom.”

That same morning the wind rose so hard down the river the keelboat had to lay to. Lewis used the hours to take the noon sun-altitude and fixed the latitude at 46°23′57″ north. They made another six miles by moonlight. The next day, October 18, was the Cannonball River. At about six miles up they came to its mouth — what the French called La Boulet, cannonball, for the perfectly round sandstone concretions the river had eroded from its banks by the thousands, “of excellent grit for Grindstons.” Clark’s men hauled one aboard as a new anchor: a river-stone for the keelboat. Two miles farther on they met two French trappers coming downriver who had been robbed near the Mandans of four traps, thirty beaver skins, an axe, guns, and ammunition. Both had been working under Gravelines, the Arikara interpreter on the boat, and they turned around and joined the party in hope that the captains’ presence would help them recover their things. Asked again about whiskey, the chief offered Clark a clean inversion of the usual Plains-trade dynamic — what Biddle would later render as: a friend would not give them what makes them fools.

October 19 was good sailing under a fair southeast wind. Clark walked the high land on the west bank and counted what he wrote down without elaboration — fifty-two gangs of buffalo and three of elk at one view, with deer and pronghorn and more than ten wolves besides. The streams falling out of the brown hills tasted of mineral salts: Glauber’s salts, sodium sulfate, the laxative; the tributary water was no longer drinkable, though the Missouri itself still was. Too Né pointed up at the conical cliffs along the river and explained where the prized tail feathers of the calumet pipe ceremonies came from — the golden eagle nested in holes the water had cut down through the soft rock. Plains diplomacy, traced back to a nest in a cliff. Late in the day, on a high point ninety feet above the plain, Clark stood among the foundations of an old fortified village and wrote in his notebook: “Here first saw ruins of Mandan nation.” Too Né said a party of Mandans had once lived there before they moved upriver.

The next day Clark walked a much larger ruin — six or eight acres of foundations on a hillside a few miles below the mouth of the Heart River. Too Né told him the Mandan had once held a string of villages on both sides of the river along this stretch, until the “Troubleson Sioux” forced them to move forty miles higher, where they had stayed only a few years before moving again. Modern history adds the partner Too Né did not name: a smallpox epidemic in 1781 had cut the Mandan population catastrophically, and the depopulated survivors — too few to defend their old towns — then yielded to Sioux pressure. The captains were tracing a retreat in slow motion. The ruin Clark walked is known today as On-a-Slant Village, the southernmost of the abandoned Mandan towns, now preserved with six reconstructed earth-lodges on the west bank just south of present Mandan, North Dakota.

On-a-Slant Village reconstructed earth-lodges, Fort Abraham Lincoln State Park, Mandan, ND. Photograph by Carol M. Highsmith. Library of Congress, Carol M. Highsmith Archive (LC-DIG-highsm-69048).

That same afternoon Pierre Cruzatte got the education the Corps had been waiting for. He fired on a “white bear” — a grizzly, the first one the expedition had met — wounded it, and was so startled by what came at him that he dropped his tomahawk and his gun and ran. The bear, fortunately, fled the other way; Cruzatte recovered his weapons. Shortly after, he shot a buffalo cow, broke her thigh, and was chased into a small ravine. Lewis recorded all of it in his natural-history notes with the dryness it deserved. They camped on the west bank above a lignite bluff, the coal Clark judged “of an inferior quality,” with the Mandan villages they were heading toward still ahead.
