Took off from Ste. Genevieve this evening to continue up the Mississippi River. Previously, I noted that the Ohio River had a lot of power plants, owing to its proximity to coal mines as well as the cooling power of the river. What I noticed on this stretch of the Mississippi was the prevalence of mines and quarries. I passed by a few on the Missouri side of the river. Apparently there are several limestone quarries along the river — and it makes sense, given that the very geological formation exposed in these bluffs is named the Ste. Genevieve Limestone, first described right here. There was even a lead smelter just north of my takeoff point — a nod to the long lead-smelting tradition of southeast Missouri that stretches back centuries.

Jefferson Barracks Limestone Quarry, St. Louis

My destination today was St. Louis Downtown Airport (KCPS), which is actually on the Illinois side of the river, in Cahokia. Lewis & Clark spent a great deal of time at the nearby Cahokia Courthouse while wintering in the area at Camp Dubois — using it as a headquarters for gathering information, meeting with territorial leaders, and corresponding with President Jefferson. The Corps spent the winter on the eastern side of the river for a very specific reason: although the Louisiana Purchase had been signed in April 1803, the territory west of the Mississippi had not yet been officially transferred to the United States. The formal handover of Upper Louisiana didn't occur until March 9–10, 1804 — a ceremony in St. Louis that Meriwether Lewis attended and witnessed in person. Although Lewis did spend time visiting St. Louis, most of his winter was spent on the Illinois (and therefore US) side of the river.

St. Louis Arch — The Gateway to the West

Before landing, I couldn't help but do a quick flyby of St. Louis to see the Gateway Arch — the monument that stands as a symbol of America's westward expansion, and in the context of this expedition, as a reminder of what this whole winter wait was about. After hunkering down on the east bank, waiting for Louisiana to officially become American soil, St. Louis was the true gateway to the unknown West for this trailblazing expedition. It felt right to circle it once before putting down.

If I'm not mistaken, this was only my second landing at a towered airport so far. Either way, I'm still working on my touch, as I fought some more ground looping on rollout. Managed to taxi away unscathed, which at this point I'll count as a win.

The Winter That Opened the West

When William Clark guided the keelboat up to the confluence of the Wood River and the Mississippi on December 12, 1803, he chose that spot with care. Just across the river, the Mississippi met the Missouri — the vast, turbulent highway the Corps would eventually ascend into the unknown. But that crossing would have to wait. The Louisiana Purchase had been signed the previous April and ratified by the Senate in October, but the land itself was still Spanish territory, and the United States could not legally occupy it. So Clark set his men to clearing timber and raising a rough-hewn stockade on the Illinois (American) side. Camp Dubois — Camp of the Woods — would be home for the next five months.

The purchase itself had been an almost accidental act of empire. President Jefferson had sent Robert Livingston and James Monroe to Paris with instructions to buy the port of New Orleans — critical for American commerce on the Mississippi — for up to $10 million. Napoleon, needing cash for his European wars and having lost Haiti to revolution, astonished them by offering the entire Louisiana Territory, roughly 828,000 square miles, for $15 million. The deal was signed April 30, 1803. At a stroke, the United States doubled in size, and the lands beyond the Mississippi — where almost no American had been — became the nation's western frontier to explore and claim.

But territory on paper and territory in hand were two different things. Lower Louisiana — New Orleans — transferred formally on December 20, 1803, while the Corps was still fighting upstream from Cairo. Upper Louisiana, centered on St. Louis, remained in Spanish hands through the winter. The formal ceremony came on March 9–10, 1804: the Spanish flag came down, the French tricolor rose briefly (France had nominally held the territory for only twenty days after Spain's November 1803 cession), and then the American flag went up over St. Louis. Three witnesses, one from each nation, signed the transfer document: Meriwether Lewis for the United States, Antoine Soulard for Spain, and Charles Gratiot for France.

Transfer of Upper Louisiana to the United States, St. Louis, March 9, 1804 — Library of Congress (public domain)

While Clark managed Camp Dubois — drilling the men, enforcing discipline over a sometimes unruly roster of soldiers, and overseeing the loading and trimming of the keelboat — Lewis ranged widely. The Cahokia Courthouse, a vertical-log French colonial building dating to around 1740 and the oldest surviving structure in Illinois, served as his informal headquarters. From there he crossed regularly to St. Louis, meeting with fur traders who had traveled the Missouri, consulting maps and accounts compiled by Spanish administrators, and writing long letters to Jefferson describing what he was learning about the country ahead. He visited the Spanish lieutenant governor, Don Carlos Dehault Delassus, and later attended the transfer ceremony that made the city American. In a very real sense, the winter at Cahokia was where the expedition's intelligence picture came into focus.

The Corps departed Camp Dubois on May 14, 1804 — Lewis, characteristically, noted it plainly: "The mouth of the River Dubois is to be considered as the point of departure." The keelboat and two pirogues pushed into the Missouri River current, and the great journey west finally began. St. Louis — still a small, largely French-speaking Creole village of perhaps a thousand souls — stood as the last outpost of the known world. Everything beyond it, from the Great Plains to the Rockies to the Pacific shore, was territory that no American expedition had yet mapped. This leg reaches that threshold. The next ones cross it.
