The Long Wished For Spot

Above the Little Missouri the country opened out into something the party had never seen at this scale. Buffalo were beginning to calve, elk were dropping their antlers, and Clark, walking ashore on April 21 to cut across a bend, came back reporting immense herds of elk, buffalo, deer and antelope with geese and ducks on every stretch of water and a few swans among them. He killed a buffalo and four deer on that one walk; the boat party added three deer, two beaver and four buffalo calves. Lewis, who kept a running critical commentary on the expedition's diet, pronounced buffalo calf the equal of any veal he had ever tasted.

The captains also made one of their more consequential naming mistakes on that stretch. The river they took for the White Earth — a name they had picked up from Hidatsa informants at Fort Mandan — was in fact the Little Muddy, and they compounded the error by hoping it ran much farther north than it does. Walking four miles up it the next day, they found the water clearer than the Missouri and the banks whitened with mineral salt, and speculated that it might head near latitude 50°, close to the Saskatchewan. That was wishful cartography rather than observation: a stream reaching that far north would have handed the United States a natural entryway into the British fur country. From the top of Cut Bluff, Lewis looked out over a plain empty of timber and thick with game, and described it as one boundless common pasture. Clark, looking at the same view, called it enchanting.

The game was not merely abundant but strangely unbothered. Walking the shore that evening, Lewis was adopted by a lone buffalo calf that fell in at his heels and followed him until he re-embarked — spooked by Seaman, Lewis guessed, and attaching itself to the nearest available protector. The same day, Clark watched wolves work a herd until they cut out a calf that could not keep pace, and noted that the cows defended their young only so long as the calves could stay with the herd. Both scenes describe the same country from opposite ends.

Then the wind took over. On April 23 it rose so violently by mid-morning that the boats could barely reach tolerable shelter on a stretch of bank with no timber on either side; canoes shipped water, cargo had to be opened and aired, and the party sat until five in the afternoon before reloading. April 24 was worse — wind-bound the entire day, waves wetting goods in the small canoes even in the lee of high timber. The real affliction was the sand. Lewis wrote at length about it: blown off the sandbars in quantities that could hide the far bank entirely and rose in the distance in columns resembling smoke, until sore eyes were a common complaint through the whole party and the men were compelled to eat, drink and breathe it freely. His own pocket watch had begun losing time, and after finding nothing mechanically wrong he concluded that fine sand had worked its way inside the supposedly tight double case. Both captains, unusually, wrote the same judgment on the same days: the wind had become a serious source of detention.

It was that frustration, as much as ambition, that sent Lewis overland. On April 25, convinced the Yellowstone was close and tired of a river so crooked that the party could rarely get three straight miles of fair wind, he set off at eleven in the morning with four men to reach the junction ahead of the boats and fix its position by observation, sparing the main party a delay later. Seaman, missing overnight, had turned up at eight that morning to Lewis's evident relief. Four miles out they met buffalo, killed a yearling calf and cooked it on the spot; from the hills afterward Lewis had what he called a most pleasing view of the country, the two wide valleys running toward each other with their meanders showing wherever the trees let them. He could not actually see the junction, hidden in timber, but camped that night on the bank of the Yellowstone about two miles above its mouth.

The next day the expedition arrived. Lewis sent Joseph Field to scout up the Yellowstone and walked to the confluence himself; around noon he heard shooting at the junction and knew the boats were in. Clark's party had fired on a buffalo herd on arrival. By evening everyone was camped together on the point of land between the two rivers, in good health and, in Lewis's phrase, much pleased at having reached this long wished for spot. The captains ordered a dram issued to every man, which — as Lewis put it — soon produced the fiddle, and the evening went to music, singing and dancing, the party seeming to forget the toils behind them and decline to think about those ahead. Field came back having gone eight miles up the Yellowstone, with a large bighorn horn picked up on the prairie. Clark measured both rivers: the Missouri 520 yards across with 330 yards of water, the Yellowstone 858 yards counting its sandbar, the one rising and the other already falling toward summer level.

Clark thought the point would make a commanding situation for a fort, and on April 27 the two captains spent the morning disagreeing about exactly where. Lewis walked the tongue of land and picked the high plain at the lower end of a small lake about two and a half miles up the Missouri — four hundred yards from one river, twice that from the other. Clark preferred the lower end of the low plain, nearer both. Lewis thought that ground too low for anything meant to last, since the rivers were capricious and versatile and would eventually cut the narrow part of the plain away and take the fortification with it. History declined to settle it in either man's favor: Fort Union in 1828, Fort William in 1832 and Fort Buford in 1866 all went up on the north side of the Missouri instead, across the water from the point both captains had been arguing over — very likely for exactly the reason Lewis flagged. That same day, sometime around midday, the party crossed into what is now Montana, where they would remain for the next four months. Lewis also set down one of the more quietly admirable lines of the journals: that although game was abundant and gentle, they killed only what they needed, and that two good hunters could conveniently supply a regiment.

Not everything on the banks was thriving. For several days the party had been passing dead buffalo along the shore, some entire and some eaten down to skin and bone by wolves and bears — animals that had gone through the ice crossing in winter, or swum the river in a weakened state and found the far bank too steep to climb. Overhead the accounting ran the other way: bald eagles more numerous, Lewis wrote, than anywhere he had seen them, with magpies building close alongside their nests and geese nesting in the same stands of trees.

April 28 finally brought a tailwind. With a southeast breeze behind them the party made twenty-four miles under sail, breakfasting under high bluffs about fifteen miles above the Yellowstone. The signature of this stretch of river showed itself plainly: coal in great abundance, salt crusting the banks and sandbars until they appeared perfectly white as if covered in snow, and bluffs banded in dark brown, yellow, light brown and dark red where seams of lignite and colored earth alternated. Clark walked the high ground with Charbonneau, killed a deer and a goose and saw three black bears. Lewis noted a tree nearly three feet through felled by beaver, and the antelope now half-changed in coat, reddish along the back and sides and snow-white behind. Four brown bears were seen that day, one fired on and wounded but not recovered. The party had been hearing about these animals since Fort Mandan and had so far mostly watched them run.
