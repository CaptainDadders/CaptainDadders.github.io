Caw Point, the confluence of the Missouri and Kansas, where the expedition stayed for several days

After hanging out in Kansas City for a day, it was time to make some more progress up the Missouri River. After leaving the Kansas City area, it was more farmland. I passed the Little Platte River on the right, and Leavenworth and Fort Leavenworth on the left. I was then very excited to fly over Lewis and Clark Lake, on the shores of which are Lewis and Clark Village, and Lewis and Clark State Park.

Lewis and Clark Lake, dead ahead

This part of the country must have special affection for our heroes, for they traveled here on the Fourth of July, 1804. As the crew fired the swivel gun, it marked the first Independence Day celebration west of the Mississippi River.

Independence Creek ahead, Atchison, KS on the left

Near Atchison, Kansas, they came across a creek that was yet unnamed, so they named it Independence Creek. The crew also enjoyed an extra gill of whiskey as part of the celebration.

Landing was in a field next to the river and surrounded by farmland. Had a crosswind and came in at quite an angle, but touchdown was smooth, and no ground-looping. I was racing a car driving next to the runway (the car beat me).

What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — June 29 – July 4, 1804

The First Fourth of July West of the Mississippi

The Corps left Kaw Point on the evening of June 29 — but not before the river’s moral climate matched its physical one. The morning had begun with a court martial: Private John Collins, posted as sentinel over the whiskey barrel, had been found drunk at his post and had allowed Private Hugh Hall to draw from it while he slept. Collins received 100 lashes on his bare back; Hall, who had pleaded guilty, received 50. Clark noted the party was “verry ready to punish Such Crimes.” Within hours of departure, the keelboat’s stern swung to within six inches of a sawyer — a submerged log anchored in the riverbed and lunging with the current, one of the most feared hazards on the Missouri. Had the bow struck it, Clark wrote, the vessel “must have Sunk in the Deep water.”

The days that followed were a catalog of what the Missouri could do. The thermometer hit 96° on June 30 as the expedition crept past the Little Platte River. The men stopped for three hours at noon, “becom verry feeble” from the heat, then returned to the oars. That evening, bringing the keelboat to shore, the mast struck an overhanging tree and snapped — the second mast casualty of the journey, the first having come three weeks earlier near Jefferson City. There was at least consolation in the hunting: nine deer taken in a single day, the animals so thick along the banks that Clark wrote their tracks were “as plenty as Hogs about a farm.”

On July 1, a mile-long sandbar off the Leavenworth shore was blanketed in driftwood — logs released when an island somewhere upstream had caved and shed its timber into the current. The next day brought something worse: a flood of drift so sudden and dense that Clark concluded an entire raft of logs had broken free at once, filling the channel. Then a sandbar so powerful that twenty oars and poles could barely hold the keelboat against the current.

That same noon the expedition halted for four hours — partly for the heat, partly to rig a temporary mast from whatever timber was available, so the crew could take advantage of the south wind blowing at their backs. By evening the improvised spar had turned, Clark noted, “a butifull red Colour,” the fresh cottonwood oxidizing in the July air.

July 4, 1804 fell just fifteen months after the Louisiana Purchase had transferred this territory from France to the United States. The Corps marked the occasion as soldiers must: a discharge from the bow swivel gun ushered in the morning, another closed the day, and every man drew an extra gill of whiskey. A nameless creek on the left bank — about fifteen yards wide, flowing out of an extensive tallgrass prairie — was given a name on the spot: Independence Creek, near present Atchison, Kansas. It was the first Fourth of July celebration west of the Mississippi River.

Private Joseph Field, walking the bank, was bitten on the foot by a snake and treated promptly by Lewis with Peruvian bark. Clark named the prairie “Jo. Fields Snake Prairie,” then looked up from his journal and wrote one of the most lyrical passages of the entire expedition. The plains, he recorded, were “covered with a Leek Green Grass, well calculated for the sweetest and most norushing hay — interspersed with Cops of trees, Spreding their lofty branchs over Pools Springs or Brooks of fine water. Groops of Shrubs covered with the most delicious froot is to be seen in every direction.” It was a country, he wrote, “far removed from the Sivilised world to be enjoyed by nothing but the Buffalo Elk Deer & Bear.” Ordway, for once, agreed with everything: “one of the most beautiful places I ever Saw in my life.”
