Another relatively long flight today, necessitated by the sparseness of airfields in the area. It was pretty scenic today; I had the feeling the river here is still similar to what it was back in 1805. At the beginning of the flight, there were fairly high bluffs along the river.

Bluffs along the Missouri

Eventually, the terrain opened up a bit, and I came to Decision Point, Montana. This is where the Marias River meets the Missouri. Back in 1805, this junction required a big decision. Which way to go? Each Captain scouted one of the rivers, before they eventually decided (correctly, in retrospect) to go left and follow the Missouri.

Marias River on the right, Missouri on the left

I also made the correct decision and took the left fork. Shortly after that, I arrived in Fort Benton, Montana. This part of the river is popular today for kayak and canoe trips. It is said to be one of the longest stretches of the river that most closely match the vibe of the 1805 adventure. I plan to paddle along this stretch of river some day.

The conditions for my landing were rather windy. I was expecting a mostly straight headwind but ended up with a pretty good crosswind as well. This made the landing interesting, but I got it down safely.

What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — May 25 – Jun 12, 1805

Which River Is the Missouri?

Through the last week of May the Corps is deep in the Missouri Breaks, hauling on the tow line. On May 25 Drouillard kills the expedition's first bighorn sheep, and Lewis fills pages describing it — the horns, the split lip, the sea-green eye, the way the animals bound along precipices where neither wolf nor bear can follow. The next morning Clark climbs the river hills and sees, for the first time with certainty, the snow-covered points of the Rocky Mountains standing above the horizon; Lewis goes up in the afternoon and sees them too. He writes of the secret pleasure of finding himself so near the head of a river he had thought boundless — and then, in the next breath, of what that snow will cost him and his men. He resolves to believe it a good comfortable road until compelled to believe otherwise.

Clark's sketch of a bighorn sheep, drawn in his journal on May 25, 1805. Missouri Historical Society; digital image via the Journals of the Lewis and Clark Expedition, University of Nebraska–Lincoln.

The men pay for every mile of it. The tow cords are elk skin, worn thin, soaked and then baked by the sun, and they part repeatedly at exactly the rocky points where a parted rope means a boat swung broadside onto stone. On May 31 the men are in cold water to their armpits a quarter of the day and dragging canoes barefoot over fallen rock, because the mud is too slippery to keep a moccasin on. That same day the river carries them into the White Cliffs, and Lewis — who has been logging mileages and rock types for a week — suddenly writes like a man walking through a ruin, describing eroded sandstone as columns, pedestals, capitals and parapets, and confessing that the scenes of visionary enchantment seemed as if they would never have an end.

Then, late on June 2, the country opens and the river forks. Two considerable streams, one 362 yards wide and one 200, and no way to tell in the failing light which of them the party has been following. Whitehouse puts it in a sentence: they camped at a fork and could not determine which was the Missouri.

It is worth being exact about what was and was not settled here, because the question is more interesting than it first looks. Nothing on any map answered it. No white man had stood at this junction. The Hidatsa at Fort Mandan had described a Missouri that ran past a great falls below the mountains and reached toward the Columbia — and had never once mentioned that it split in two. So in the narrowest sense, yes: the choice made the name. There was no prior authority to consult, and when Lewis christened the north fork Maria's River on June 8, that act is what left the south fork holding the name Missouri. But that framing flatters the decision by making it sound arbitrary, and it was not. Underneath the naming lay a plain question of fact with a right answer: which branch led to the falls, into the mountains, and on toward the Columbia. Naming the wrong river would have cost nothing. Taking it would have cost the season, and Lewis believed it might have cost the expedition.

The evidence pointed both ways, which is the whole trouble. The south fork ran clear and rapid over a bed of round smooth stones, the way rivers out of mountains do. The north fork ran deep, thick, brownish and turbid, boiling and rolling in exactly the manner the Missouri has had for 2,500 miles. To the men that settled it — the north fork simply was the river they had been fighting since St. Louis, and the party, with very few exceptions, pronounced for it. Lewis read the same water backwards: mud means a long run through open plains, and a stream that had come any distance out of the mountains would run clear. Clear water meant mountains. That argument decided the expedition.

Neither captain would take it on argument alone. On June 4 they split — Lewis up the Marias with six men, Clark up the south fork with five. Clark went far enough to watch his river hold its width, depth and southwesterly course, and turned back on the 5th because more walking would tell him nothing new. Lewis went about sixty miles and watched his river bend gradually north, which told him everything. Coming back down in freezing rain on the 7th he slipped on wet gumbo above a ninety-foot drop and saved himself with his espontoon, then heard Richard Windsor cry out behind him, flat on his belly with one arm and one leg over the edge. Lewis, by his own account much alarmed, disguised his feelings entirely, told the man in a calm voice that he was in no kind of danger, and talked him through cutting a foothold in the bank with his knife. Both captains reached the same conclusion on June 6, by opposite roads, and neither knew the other had.

Lewis names the river on the 8th, for his cousin Maria Wood, allowing that the hue of so turbid a stream ill comports with that lovely fair one's virtues, and predicting it will one day be contested between the United States and Great Britain. He notes plainly that every man in the party except himself is still fully persuaded this is the Missouri. On the 9th he lays the whole case out for them, and the men give the finest answer in the journals: they still think the officers are wrong, and they are ready to follow them anywhere. Cruzatte, the old Missouri waterman whose judgment on water everyone in camp trusts, says flatly that the north fork is the true Missouri and can be no other. That evening Cruzatte gets out his fiddle and the whole camp dances until late.

Then they make the decision expensive. On the 10th they dig a cache in high dry ground, bury a thousand pounds of tools, powder, provisions and superfluous baggage, draw the red pirogue up on an island and brand the trees beside her. Sacagawea falls sick the same evening and worsens. On the 11th Lewis walks off up the south fork with four men to settle the wager the only way it can be settled — by going to look for the falls the Hidatsa described. He gets nine miles before intestinal pain and fever stop him cold, boils chokecherry twigs into a black bitter decoction for want of any medicine, and is well enough by morning to make twenty-seven miles. From a ridge on June 12 he sees range behind range, each rising higher than the last, the farthest losing its snowy tops in the clouds: an august spectacle, he writes, and rendered still more formidable by the recollection that they had them to pass. Eighteen miles downstream, Clark works the boats up past the black clay bluffs below present Fort Benton with his interpreter moved out of the sun into the shade of the pirogue's awning, too ill to sit up.
