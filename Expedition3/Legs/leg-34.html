What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — Apr 9 – 14, 1805

The Great Bend and the White Bear

Six days out of Fort Mandan the Corps was learning what the country ahead would cost them. The Mandan man who had promised to guide them as far as the Shoshones changed his mind over breakfast on the first morning and walked back to his village — a small thing, but the first sign of how little the captains' arrangements were worth once the party moved beyond the reach of the villages. From here on the guarantees would have to be made again, with strangers, one nation at a time.

What they gained instead was country. The river swung west at the Great Bend and the plains opened into what Lewis called one continuous level fertile plain as far as the eye could reach, timber surviving only in the folds where prairie fire could not reach it. The bluffs were striated with seams of what he termed carbonated wood — lignite, a hundred feet up the banks, and one of them burning as they passed, throwing off sulphurous smoke. Salt crusts whitened the hillsides and made the springs undrinkable. The Corps was passing through a landscape that behaved like nothing in their experience, and Lewis wrote it all down: the two color phases of snow geese, an unfamiliar burrowing animal, geese nesting improbably high in cottonwood trees, a jackrabbit caught mid-change from winter white, a creeping juniper he pressed and admired as a plant that would edge a garden handsomely.

They were not the only ones moving. Three French trappers who had left Fort Mandan days ahead of them had already taken twelve beaver — the finest pelts Lewis had ever seen — and asked to travel under the party's protection as far as the Yellowstone. The fur trade was following the expedition west before the expedition had gotten anywhere. Hidatsa hunting camps watched the river for the pronghorn crossing; a party of Hidatsa returning from a winter with the Assiniboine appeared on the south shore with loaded horses and dogs; abandoned lodges stood along both banks by the dozen. This was emphatically inhabited country, hunted so hard between Fort Mandan and the Little Missouri that Drouillard's deer, when it finally came, was the first fresh meat in days and made what Lewis called a comfortable dinner of venison steak, beaver tails, and biscuit salvaged from a soaking. The wetted gunpowder from the same accident turned out almost restored — a loss much smaller than they had feared.

Twice in three days the river nearly took the whole enterprise. The loaded red pirogue was towed beneath a rapidly collapsing bank and came through only just; Lewis admitted it cost him some moments of uneasiness, given what her cargo meant in their present advanced situation. Then, running under sail with a fair wind, a squall laid the white pirogue over. Charbonneau had the helm, panicked, and turned her broadside to the wind, and the jibing spritsail brought her as near to oversetting as Lewis thought it possible to miss. Drouillard took the tiller and the sails came down. Lewis set out afterward exactly what had been two hundred yards from shore in high water: the instruments, the papers, the medicine, the best of the trade goods, and Sacagawea with her infant and three men who could not swim. It is the plainest inventory in the journals of how much of the expedition was carried in a single boat, and how thin the margin was.

At the mouth of the Little Missouri they stopped a full day to take observations, and Lewis wrote a long assessment of the new river — its width, its difficult navigation, its resemblance to the Missouri in color and current — reasoning outward from it toward the character of the country upstream. Jean Baptiste Lepage, an engagé who had once descended that river by canoe in forty-five days, supplied the correction that moved the Knife and Little Missouri to their proper places on the captains' map. This is what the early upper river looked like as a working problem: guesses, refined by whoever aboard had been there. And here Sacagawea's own value first showed in a form the journals bothered to record, when she went out at a dinner halt and dug wild artichoke tubers from mouse caches, gathering a good quantity.

The Assiniboine were everywhere in evidence and nowhere in person. Clark's shore walks turned up single lodges, two large recent encampments, and the remains of forty-three lodges near a shallow lake he named Goose Egg after shooting a goose nesting in a cottonwood above it. Among the camp debris were hoops from small liquor kegs — proof enough for the captains, since no other people on this stretch of river drank spirits. Lewis wrote at length on their trade with the British posts on the Assiniboine River: dried and pounded buffalo meat, grease, wolf and fox furs, exchanged for kegs of rum, and drunkenness held among the men as a matter of exultation rather than shame. Clark set down a careful description of the skin lodge itself — buffalo hides sewn together and raised on a pole frame, decorated with tails and porcupine quills, left open at the smoke hole. A dog the Assiniboine had left behind attached itself to the party and stayed.

“A Skin Lodge of an Assiniboin Chief,” Karl Bodmer, from Maximilian’s atlas. Rare Book Division, The New York Public Library. Bodmer traveled the upper Missouri in 1832–34, roughly a generation after the Corps found the abandoned Assiniboine camps described here.

Then the tracks. Among buffalo that had drowned falling through the winter ice, the shoreline was printed with the feet of an animal the party had heard about all winter and not yet seen. Lewis wrote down what the Hidatsa had told him: that they hunted the white bear only in parties of six to ten, that they lost men even so — two the previous winter — and that a man prepared for the hunt with the same ceremonies he would use before going to war. The Corps' own confidence was still intact; they had good rifles and no experience. Near the end of this stretch Lewis fired at an elk and Clark, watching from the hills, saw two of them break and run at the sound — too far off to judge color or size, but no longer only tracks.

They marked the boundary that same day. A creek was named for Charbonneau, who had once camped there for weeks with a Hidatsa hunting party, and by the captains' own reckoning it stood at the farthest point up the Missouri any white man had reached, excepting two Frenchmen who had strayed slightly past it while lost. Everything upriver was new.
