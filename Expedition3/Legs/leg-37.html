After a bit of a hiatus I am back in the sky. Took some time off for real-life adventures, including a trip to Montana, where I saw some of the upcoming sights of this journey myself. More on that when I reach the Great Falls area. But for now, I am in northeastern Montana. The land remains very sparsely populated. I passed the small towns of Poplar and Wolf Point, which are in the Fort Peck Indian Reservation. Other than that, it was a lot of seemingly empty land.

The winding Missouri River in eastern Montana

The river took a winding path along this stretch, and I cheated a bit and flew straight over many of the curves. This shortened my distance considerably from what Lewis and Clark had to travel.

Finally, I reached Fort Peck Dam, the highest dam along the Missouri River. I landed near the dam at Fort Peck Airport (37S). The landing was smooth, until I pulled up too early and lifted the plane back into the air. I'll blame that on being out of practice.

What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — Apr 29 – May 9, 1805

Grizzly Country

On the morning of April 29 Lewis walked ashore with one man and fell in with two brown bears. Both were hit; one got away, and the other turned and ran him seventy or eighty yards before a second ball finished it. It was a male, not full grown, perhaps three hundred pounds — the first grizzly the Corps had in hand, and the basis for Lewis's first description of the species. His verdict was confident: the Indians may well fear the animal, armed as they are with bows and indifferent fuzees, but “in the hands of skillfull riflemen they are by no means as formidable or dangerous as they have been represented.” That same day Clark watched a bighorn ewe and her lamb come off a bluff face that was nearly perpendicular, the first the party had seen. Both animals had been described to them all winter at Fort Mandan. They camped at the mouth of a stream Clark named Martha’s River, “in honor to the Selebrated M. F.” Nobody has ever established who she was.

The country was giving more than the party could use. Walking the shore the next day, Sacagawea brought Clark a currant bush in bloom and told him the fruit was delicious and grew in great quantity in the Rocky Mountains; Ordway watched buffalo swimming across ahead of the canoes and let them pass, having meat enough aboard. Then the weather took the schedule away. The wind pinned them down through May 1 and blew all night, turning to snow at daylight — an inch deep on the level, drifted a foot against the sandbars, falling on cottonwood leaves already as large as a dollar. Both captains reached for the same word for it: singular. Ice froze to the oars that evening, and the next morning a kettle left near the fire had a quarter inch on it.

Two rivers got named on May 3, both for the plainest reasons available. The clear stream from the north became Porcupine River, for the number of them at its mouth — the first tributary Lewis had seen enter the Missouri with transparent water. A creek a quarter mile above became 2,000 Mile Creek, for the distance from the Missouri’s mouth. Lewis guessed Porcupine rose near the Saskatchewan and might open a road to the Athabasca fur country, a reminder that this was a commercial reconnaissance as much as a scientific one.

The abundance had made the animals careless. On May 4 Lewis passed bull buffalo in the open plain within fifty paces: “they viewed me for a moment as something novel and then very unconcernedly continued to feed.” The correction came the following evening, when a grizzly was spotted on a sand beach and Clark went out with Drouillard. It took ten balls, five through the lungs; the bear swam more than half the width of the river and was at least twenty minutes dying. They measured it — eight feet seven and a half inches nose to hind feet, claws four and three-eighths inches — and could not agree on its weight, Clark guessing five hundred pounds and Lewis judging that low by a hundred. Its heart, Lewis wrote, was as large as an ox’s. When another bear swam past the boats the next day and got clear, he admitted the party’s curiosity was pretty well satisfied: the difficulty of killing them even when shot through the vitals had staggered the resolution of several of the men. That was six days after his own sentence about skillful riflemen.

They reached a large river from the north on May 8. Its water carried a peculiar whiteness — about the color, Lewis wrote, of a cup of tea with a tablespoonful of milk in it — and from that they called it Milk River, which it still is. The captains also recognized it as the stream the Hidatsa had described over the winter as the river which scolds at all others, and they were right. Sacagawea gathered wild licorice and breadroot on the hillsides that day and gave them to Clark, which set Lewis off on one of the fullest plant descriptions in the journals. The undertone was quieter: the party found a pronghorn hide someone had recently scraped, and Clark thought he made out smoke far up the Milk. Lewis noted they would rather not meet those gentlemen just now.

May 9 produced the strangest bed of the stretch — a channel as wide as the Missouri itself, about half a mile, holding standing pools and not a drop of running water. Lewis walked up it, worked out that it drained a great extent of dry plain and ran only on the spring melt, and named it Big Dry River. That evening Charbonneau made his boudin blanc, and Lewis, who rarely had a kind word for the man, called it one of the greatest delicacies of the forest and wrote out the recipe with obvious pleasure. He closed on a different note: “I begin to feel extreemly anxious to get in view of the rocky mountains.” Eleven days had cost them roughly 215 river miles of bends and doubling back, against about 143 statute miles for this leg’s course, which follows the same general line of the river but cuts across many of the bends. They camped that night at the mouth of a creek named for William Werner — ground that now lies under Fort Peck Reservoir, along with most of what Lewis walked that day.
