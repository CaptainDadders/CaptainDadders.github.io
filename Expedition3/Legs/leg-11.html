Today I continued flying up the Mississippi River. For Lewis and Clark, this stretch began the difficult part of their journey, as they were now traveling upstream. I had the good fortune of a tailwind for this flight.

Captain Lewis described many islands in the river along this stretch, some of which were camping spots for the expedition. I flew over the same islands today, with rain clouds in the distance.

Islands in the Mississippi River

I passed only a few small towns on this stretch of the river, the largest being Cape Girardeau, Missouri. Today its population is around forty thousand. When Lewis and Clark stopped here in 1803, there would have been only a few settlers.

My landing spot today was a small grass strip next to a farm: Lambdins Field (12LL), on the Illinois side of the river. My quest for a perfect landing will have to continue — today brought more bouncing and a little ground looping.

What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — Nov 20–24, 1803

A Visit to the Red House

After nearly a week camped at the mouth of the Ohio taking celestial observations to fix their position on the map, the Corps turned upstream into the Mississippi on November 20, 1803. The river ran almost flat-out the other way. What this flight covered in thirty-three minutes took the keelboat and pirogues four hard days, with camps each night on islands and along the wooded banks.

They were also now inside the Louisiana Purchase — the vast tract France had sold the United States that spring, though the formal handover ceremonies at New Orleans and St. Louis were still weeks and months away. On November 23 the expedition reached Cape Girardeau, the first non-American settlement they had encountered since leaving Pittsburgh. A French soldier named Jean Baptiste de Girardot had built a short-lived trading post on the rock bluff above the river around 1733, giving the place its name. By 1803 the district was governed for the Spanish crown by a French-Canadian commandant named Louis Lorimier, whose trading post — the Red House — was reputed to be the largest between St. Louis and Memphis.

Clark was unwell that day and took the keelboat upriver to set camp. Lewis came ashore with letters of introduction and found Lorimier in the middle of a horse race. The journal entry from that evening is one of Lewis's longest of the entire downriver passage. He sketched Lorimier in vivid detail: about sixty years old, French-Canadian by birth, with hair worn in a single braid “nearly as low as his knees” and held at the waist by a leather girdle. Lorimier's wife Charlotte was Shawnee. Their daughter, Lewis wrote, was “much the most descent looking feemale” he had seen since leaving Kentucky.

There was an unspoken backstory to the meeting. In the early 1780s — during the Revolutionary War — William Clark's older brother, General George Rogers Clark, had led the raid that burned Lorimier's Ohio trading post to the ground, costing him an estimated twenty thousand dollars. Lorimier had rebuilt his fortunes on the Spanish side of the Mississippi and, by Lewis's account, “treated me with much politeness in his way.” After supper at the Red House, one of Lorimier's sons rode upriver with Lewis to rejoin Clark and the camped Corps. St. Louis was still about three weeks of fighting current away.
