On August 25, 1804, Lewis & Clark set out with Lewis's dog Seaman and several of the men on a side trip to Spirit Mound. The Native Americans had told them they were afraid to climb this mound because it was inhabited by evil spirits who would attack anyone who approached. Lewis & Clark, therefore, decided they must climb to the top. Today, I re-created their journey as best I could, by air.

Vermillion River (known as White Stone Creek in 1804)

I flew to the mouth of the Vermillion River, which was then known as the White Stone Creek. This is a pretty small creek, and it flows alongside the town of Vermillion. I then flew North, paralleling South Dakota State Highway 19, until I reached Spirit Mound.

It was not much to see in the sim. I could see the trail to its summit, but it was not very tall at all as I flew over it. I imagine it is somewhat more impressive in real life. Here is a view from the plane, followed by a modern photograph of Spirit Mound.

I circled the mound, headed back to the river, and then flew upstream to the next airport at Yankton, SD (KYKN). Even with the circling of the mound and a go around at Yankton, the total flight time was 36 minutes. Lewis & Clark, on the other hand, started at 8am, got back to their boat at sunset, and then had to paddle upstream in the dark and rain to get to their campsite.

Reading the journals from Lewis & Clark and the other expedition members, it made me ponder how vastly different this journey was in 1804 than it would be for someone who wanted to climb to the top of the mound today. I asked Captain Claude to write about the contrasts.

What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — August 25–27, 1804

A Most Beautiful Landscape

On the morning of August 25, 1804, a sweltering Saturday, Lewis and Clark and ten other men dropped down the Missouri to the mouth of the White Stone Creek, left two men with the white pirogue, and set out across the prairie to climb a small conical hill that the Omaha, Sioux, and Otoe peoples called the Mountain of Little People. The neighboring nations would not approach it; they believed it was inhabited by eighteen-inch devils with sharp arrows who killed any man who dared come close. Clark's explanation for the legend, which he worked out from the summit itself later that day, was characteristically rationalist: the wind on the open plain funneled flying insects against the isolated mound, insectivorous birds gathered to feed on them, and the constant assemblage of birds over an otherwise unremarkable hill was striking enough to seed a tradition of spirits.

The walk was nine miles in heat that would read 86 degrees in the shade beside the river that afternoon — the only thermometer reading recorded between mid-May and mid-September of 1804, an accidental measurement of an extraordinary day. Lewis was still weakened from inhaling cobalt fumes three days earlier and pushed himself hard. Seaman, Lewis's Newfoundland, gave out at about the seventh mile and was sent back to the creek alone. Several men were complaining of great thirst by the time they reached the summit at noon, where they found no devils — only three holes in the ground that Clark assumed were coyote or badger dens, and the predicted cloud of small brown swallows working through swarms of harvester ants on the lee side of the hill. The ants stung Clark sharply on the neck while he tried to take bearings.

But the view. From a hill of seventy feet they could see clear to the horizon in every direction, and Clark wrote: "we beheld a most butifull landscape; Numerous herds of buffalow were Seen feeding in various directions, the Plain to North N. W & N E extends without interuption as far as Can be Seen." He estimated upwards of eight hundred buffalo and elk grazing within sight. No woods anywhere except in the river bottoms. No human-built object in any direction. The captains were standing on one of the last vantage points on the continent from which the unaltered Great Plains could be seen as a single, uninterrupted thing.

What ought to have been a triumphant survey nearly ended badly. Lewis was failing in the heat; the men were desperate for water; they were forced to break off and march three miles northeast to the nearest creek, where they drank from a beaver-dammed pool and rested for an hour and a half. The return trip back to the pirogue took the rest of the daylight. They reached the boat at sunset, with York, the only enslaved member of the Corps, "nearly exosted with heat thurst and fatigue" by Clark's curt measure. Finding that the keelboat under Sergeant Pryor had already moved on six miles upriver as ordered, they chose to row the white pirogue back upstream to their previous campsite rather than push on through the rain and darkness. Supper that night was jerked meat and two prairie larks, eaten over a fire under the rain, with the men sleeping on a buffalo robe.

The mound the captains climbed is one of the few documented Lewis and Clark sites in the country where a visitor today can stand on the exact ground they stood on. Most of the campsites along the lower Missouri are now submerged behind the six dams authorized by the Flood Control Act of 1944; the river itself flows in channels they would not recognize. The mound, being a hill on the prairie, could not be drowned. It is preserved today as the 320-acre Spirit Mound Historic Prairie, managed by the state of South Dakota; a 1.4-mile graveled trail leads from a parking lot off Highway 19 up to the summit and back. The walk takes an hour. The site was a cattle feedlot until 2001; restoration of the original prairie is ongoing and will take decades more before the grasses approximate what the Corps walked through.

To climb the hill today is therefore an experience that is, by every measurable standard, easier than it was in 1804 — and at the same time, by every measurable standard, poorer. Easier: the route is signed, the trail is wide, water is in the car, the elevation gain is modest, and the summit is reached in twenty minutes rather than four hours of cross-country navigation by sighting a cone on the horizon. Poorer: the landscape Clark surveyed from the top no longer exists. The plain that ran without interruption to the north and northwest and northeast is now divided into square fields by section-line roads, ringed with farmsteads and silos, threaded with power lines and the town of Vermillion. The herds of eight hundred buffalo and elk are not there; the wild bison were extirpated from South Dakota by the late nineteenth century. The water in the Vermillion River, which the Corps drank without hesitation from a beaver dam in 1804, has not been potable along any stretch of its modern course for a very long time. What was wild is not wild. What was open is not open. What could be drunk cannot be drunk.

A small handful of the August 25 detail probably is still the same, if a visitor goes on the right kind of afternoon. The harvester ants almost certainly still swarm at the summit when the wind is up; they are native, they prefer this habitat, and there is no reason they would not still be there. The swallows that hunt them are also still common in the area. Lewis recorded that the birds were so absorbed in their feeding that they would not leave the lee of the hill until the men were within a few feet of them: "they were So gentle that they did not quit the place untill we had arrivd. within a fiew feet of them." The smallest creatures in his account are the ones that may have survived best. The buffalo are gone. The prairie around them is being rebuilt by hand. The river they came up has been impounded. But on a hot day in late August, the wind still drives the insects into the hill, and the swallows still come.

The Corps did not linger over the mound. The captains returned to the boat the morning of August 26 and made another nine miles upriver, and that day Patrick Gass was formally appointed sergeant to fill the vacancy left by Charles Floyd's death six days earlier — the institutional closing of the only death the expedition would ever suffer. On August 27, two miles after they set sail in a gentle southeast breeze, they passed a long bluff of white chalk on the south bank and at the mouth of the James River met their first plains-nomad Sioux: a young Yankton man swam out to the pirogue, and two boys followed. Sergeant Pryor and the old French interpreter Pierre Dorion were sent off to the Sioux camp on the James to invite the chiefs to council at the Calumet Bluff upriver. The prairie fires the captains had set as signals two days earlier had finally been answered, and the long passage among the nations of the upper Missouri was about to begin.
