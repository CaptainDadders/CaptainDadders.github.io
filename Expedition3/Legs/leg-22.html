After a couple of days in Omaha, it was time to head back to the river. Looking back after takeoff, it struck me just how big this airport is compared to most of the landing strips I've visited... and how perfectly framed it is by the Missouri River.

Leaving Omaha

Today's leg was deceptively short. My navigator, Captain Claude, predicted the flight would take 51 minutes. It took 29. I believe Claude bases its estimates on the original expedition path. Looking at the map, you can see that the river was quite a bit twistier in 1804 than it is in 2026. Since I followed the river as it flows today, my route was shorter than it would have been for Lewis and Clark.

While the flight today was short, there was a lot going on for this leg of the expedition. Along this section of the journey, Lewis and Clark held their first council with Native Americans, at a bluff along the Missouri River near what would eventually become Fort Atkinson. The river has since changed course and does not flow directly next to Fort Atkinson, but I ventured away from the river to fly by this important historical site. After comparing against satellite map imagery, I'm pretty sure the spiral path out the left side of the cockpit window is the location of the First Council Commemorative Bronze Statues. The First Council

For five days the Corps waited beneath a high bluff on the west bank, a flag run up a fresh-cut pole, while nothing happened. Clark had climbed that bluff the afternoon they arrived and come down convinced it was the finest ground he'd ever seen — high prairie, rich soil, the river bending away between distant ranges of hills, and a clay bank that would, he thought, make a commanding spot for a fort someday. But the men they'd come to meet were out on the summer buffalo hunt, scattered across the plains, and an invitation sent ahead had vanished along with the Frenchman who carried it. So they waited: shooting deer, swatting at the bottoms' relentless mosquitoes, and skinning a badger — a creature none of them had encountered before — which Lewis carefully preserved to ship back east. Clark turned thirty-four and marked it with a dinner of venison and beavertail.

The Otoe and Missouria came at sunset on August 2, firing their guns as they appeared. The Corps answered with the cannon, and the two sides spent the evening eating roasted meat and watermelon, the real business held over for morning. What the captains staged the next day under an awning of the boat's mainsail was meant to be nothing less than the announcement of a new world: the land had a new ruler, the distant American president in Washington whom Lewis called their “great father,” the old French and Spanish traders were Americans now, and peace and trade would flow up the river on American terms. They handed out medals stamped with Jefferson's face and commissions ranking the chiefs, and sent the grand chief's medal off after him, since Little Thief was still away hunting.

The speech had a hook inside it. Make peace with your neighbors and trade with the Americans, Lewis promised, and the traders would come — a post at the mouth of the Platte, goods on far better terms than the French had ever offered. But ignore the new father, listen to the “bad birds,” and the trade would simply stop, and there would be suffering. It was a threat dressed as generosity: behave, or we leave you alone. There is a hard irony folded into those words that no one in that pavilion could have read at the time — that being left alone, cut off from the relentless westward press of trade and settlement, might have been the kinder fate of the two.

If the Otoe and Missouria felt the threat, they did not flinch at it. They were experienced diplomats who had sat through this kind of ceremony with the French and the Spanish for generations, and a change of distant fathers likely sounded like what it had always been — a reshuffling of trading partners. They made, in Clark's words, “some very sensible speeches,” accepted the gifts graciously, noted dryly that the French had never once handed them so much as a free knife, asked for a little powder and a drop of whiskey, and went back to their lives. The grand pronouncement of American sovereignty seems to have moved them about as much as the weather.

Lewis and Clark in council — engraving from the published edition of Patrick Gass's journal (Library of Congress). An early-1800s illustration, not a literal depiction of the August 3 council.

What did land was the air gun. When Lewis raised his rifle and fired it again and again — no powder, no smoke, no reloading — that was the moment the room changed. Whatever the speeches claimed about a faraway president, the gun was a thing they could see, and it didn't behave the way guns were supposed to. The captains had brought a speech to impress; it was the machine that did the impressing.

Then the river took its revenge on the whole tidy occasion. Pushing north, the Corps fought a channel choked with snags and collapsing banks, the kind of water where a point of land 370 yards across by foot cost twelve miles to round by boat. Somewhere in there Private Moses Reed slipped back “for his knife” and didn't return — the one man who would simply walk away from the expedition — and the captains, after days of doubt, resolved to send a detachment after him with orders hard enough to match the offense. On August 7 four men turned downriver to bring him in and to carry a peace message to the Otoe at the same time; the manhunt and the diplomacy rode out together. By then Sergeant Floyd had noted feeling unwell, a small line in the record that would not stay small.

Clark's instinct about the bluff proved sound — the ground was good enough that the Army would later plant its first post west of the Missouri right there. But stand on that spot now and the river is gone from beneath it. The Missouri that ran along the base of Council Bluff in 1804 has wandered some three miles east, the work of a century of restless meandering that the Army Corps of Engineers finally pinned in place with channel work in the 1930s. The bluff still looks out over the bottoms Clark admired. It just looks out over farm fields now, and the water that carried the keelboat has moved on — which, all things considered, is exactly what the Otoe and Missouria did when the speeches were over.
