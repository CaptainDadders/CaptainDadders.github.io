Another long layover, this time on the Four Bears Peninsula. Shortly after taking off, I was flying past Four Bears Village, ND. With a population of around 500, it was the most populous town I flew past today -- it was the only town I flew past today.

Four Bears Village off the left wing, and the Four Bears Bridge

After that it was a lonely flight over a too-wide Missouri River past a mostly empty landscape. When Lewis and Clark paddled past, it was a different kind of empty. No Native Americans were met along this part of the journey, but it was a landscape filled with buffalo, elk, deer, and beaver. They were also now in grizzly bear territory.

The dammed Missouri River

Landed at Lindvig Airstrip, which was just a flat empty piece of land. I almost had to guess where the airstrip was. Being a little rusty, I came in a little high, but managed to stop before I ran out of airstrip.

What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — Apr 15 – 20, 1805

Beaver Bark and Buffalo Wool

Above the Mandan villages the Corps moved through a country that was crowded and empty at the same time. People had plainly been there. Clark found an antelope pen at the mouth of a creek he named Goat Pen Creek — a stout timber pound with a narrow gate and long brush wings running out from it to funnel pronghorn inside. Old Assiniboine camps stood at nearly every wooded point along the river. Timber rafts lay abandoned on the bank, tracks in the mud were less than a day old, and a scaffold grave on the shore held a woman wrapped and laced into robes with her dog sleds, her paint bags, beaver claws and hide tools stacked beside her. What the party never found in six days was a single living person. The sign was everywhere; the makers of it were somewhere else.

Karl Bodmer, “Tombs of Assiniboin Indians on Trees,” 1832–34. Rare Book Division, The New York Public Library — public domain. Bodmer came up the Missouri roughly twenty-eight years after the expedition; the painting shows the burial tradition the party kept encountering, not this particular grave.

The animals more than filled the gap. Beaver, above all. Both captains kept returning to them in the journals with something close to astonishment: bigger, fatter, more numerous and better furred than anything they had seen on the whole river, the pelts darker than usual. The evidence was standing on the banks — cottonwoods twenty inches through, felled by teeth. Bark was the only thing they were eating on this stretch, cottonwood and willow, and they were eating well on it. The traps came up loaded night after night.

They were also eating the beaver. Lewis wrote plainly that the men preferred beaver to any other meat available, and that he ate heartily of it himself, the tail and the liver being the best parts. That enthusiasm had consequences. One morning a single beaver came up caught in two different traps at once — a hind foot in one man's, a forefoot in another's — and the argument over whose animal it was got hot enough that the captains had to walk into the middle of it before it turned into a fight. It is one of the few glimpses in the whole journal of what six days of monotony and rich trapping does to a small group of armed men.

The buffalo told a different story. The herds were immense, spread across the plains alongside elk, pronghorn and wolves, and they were miserable eating. Lewis picked out what he judged the fattest bull in a herd, shot it, found it too poor to bother with, and took only the tongue; a second animal was leaner still. Along the shore, driftwood piles held drowned buffalo that had gone through the ice over the winter, some of them half eaten by bears. The most useful thing the animals gave up that week was not meat at all. Lewis stopped to examine tufts of buffalo hair snagged on wild rose bushes and bleached white by the weather, and found it finer and softer than sheep's wool — good enough, he thought, to be made into excellent cloth. He estimated a single winter coat would yield about five pounds of it.

The wind, meanwhile, was running the schedule. It pinned the canoes against the bank for four hours one afternoon, blowing hard enough out of the north to threaten swamping them while they sat moored, and the pirogues had to be worked around outside the smaller boats to break the waves. The next day it never let up at all, and the party spent the whole of it in a sheltered timbered harbor, killing an elk and robbing goose nests for eggs. When they finally got out, a bank collapsed near one of the canoes and nearly rolled it, several boats shipped water rounding the first point, and the wet provisions had to be spread out to dry. That day's progress was six and a half miles.

Between the wind and the trapping there was time to look at things. The prairies were greening, cottonwoods leafing out, wild plum in full bloom. Lewis pressed a dwarf juniper that never grew above three feet, and a low flowering pea from the bottom lands. He worked out — correctly, as it turned out — that the scorched red bluffs along the river were the signature of coal seams that had burned underground, noting how the burnt earth and the unburned coal kept to the same height and thickness in the bank. Clark walked nine miles inland across treeless plains and found small streams draining northeast, evidence of how narrowly the Missouri and the Hudson Bay watersheds missed each other in that country. His complaint about the water was blunter: except for the Knife, the Little Missouri, and the Missouri itself, he had not found one drop fit to drink since leaving Fort Mandan.

And in among it all, the tracks. Grizzly sign was constant — on the sandbars, around the drowned carcasses, on the high ground where the men went to set traps. Twice the party saw the animals themselves, and both times the bears ran. Two men who walked into one upriver while unarmed backed off and lived to report it. Lewis had heard the Hidatsa accounts of what these bears could do and had come upriver braced for them; what he was actually seeing did not match the stories, and he said so in the journal. It was the kind of note a man writes early.
