The forecast was for a windy day today, so I got an early start, so that I could fly in calm air. I had a few sights to see today. The first was the confluence of the Niobrara with the Missouri. The Niobrara is the smaller river flowing into the Missouri, behind and to the left of the plane.

Niobrara River flowing into the Missouri

Another one of my goals was to find Old Baldy, which featured in Lewis & Clark's story. The captains climbed this peak, and the Corps happened upon a prairie dog town here, which intrigued them greatly. I managed to find Old Baldy, but, unsurprisingly, it was somewhat unimpressive in the sim. I flew around it to try to get a good view. You can see it below to the left of the plane, in front of the wing.

Then it was back to the river, and before long, another dam. I reached the Fort Randall Dam, completed in 1956. Behind it, the Missouri River flooded and became Lake Francis Case.

Fort Randall Dam and Lake Francis Case

Then it was time to land at Pepper Port "Airport", a few miles from the river. Calling this an airport is quite the stretch. When I approached, I could not make out anything resembling a runway. I ended up flying right past the apparent landing area, and made a pretty rough landing in a field. After the fact, I checked online and found at least one source saying "This airport is closed". Old Baldy and the Prairie Dog Town

By the first week of September 1804, the keelboat had pushed past the mouth of the Niobrara — a river the men called by its French name, L'Eau qui Court, "the water that runs" — and into a country that no longer looked like anything they had seen. The timber thinned to nothing outside the river bottoms. Sergeant Gass tried to put the change into words: from the top of any hill, he wrote, a person could see "as far as the eye can reach without any obstruction," nothing but open prairie rolling away in every direction. The Corps had arrived, fully, on the Great Plains.

The new country announced itself through its animals. On September 3, Clark recorded the expedition's first sighting of the pronghorn — "several wild Goats Seen in the Plains," he wrote, "they are wild & fleet." Two days later came the first mention of mule deer, the "Deer with black tales." The plains were thick with game in numbers the men struggled to convey. On September 9, walking the south bank, Clark looked up and counted "at least 500 Buffalow" in a single view, with elk and deer in every stand of timber. That evening he ordered York, the enslaved man who had come west as his servant and who hunted as a working member of the party, to bring down a buffalo near the boat.

Buffalo Bulls in a Wallow, George Catlin, 1837–39 — painted about thirty years after the expedition. Smithsonian American Art Museum, gift of Mrs. Joseph Harrison, Jr.

The most memorable day of the stretch was September 7, at the foot of a dome-shaped knob the captains called the Cupola — known by the 1830s, and ever since, as Old Baldy. It stands on the south side of the river in present Boyd County, Nebraska, near today's village of Lynch. Lewis and Clark climbed to its top, a rounded rise about seventy feet above the surrounding plain, and found the view worth the walk. But it was what waited at its base that captured the whole Corps. They had stumbled onto their first prairie dog town. Fascinated, nearly every man not standing guard fell to the task of trying to capture one, hauling kettle after kettle of water from the river and pouring it down the burrows to flush an animal out. They emptied five barrels into a single hole without filling it, and dug six feet straight down through hard clay without reaching the bottom of the den. At last they drowned one out alive. That small animal — Cynomys ludovicianus, a species new to science — would be carefully kept, and the captains set down the first scientific description of the prairie dog and its underground colonies.

The river itself was a different creature here than the one the Norden follows today. The water the Corps fought against — shallow, braided, choking with sandbars so numerous Clark said they were "not worth mentioning" — forced the men onto the towline, hauling the boat upstream by rope from the bank, the labor that would define the months ahead. That wild, shifting channel no longer exists along this reach. In 1956 the Army Corps of Engineers completed Fort Randall Dam, one link in the great Pick–Sloan chain of mid-century Missouri River dams, and the river above it backed up into Lake Francis Case — a reservoir more than a hundred miles long. The sandbars Clark cursed, the cutbanks the towline parties scrambled along, the very shape of the water the Corps measured by the day all lie drowned beneath that still pool now. As with the dam and lake passed on earlier legs, the wild Missouri the expedition knew survives here only in their words.

One thread runs unresolved through the whole week. Private George Shannon, the youngest man in the Corps, had been missing since late August, somewhere ahead of the boat on foot. Colter, sent to find him, rejoined the keelboat on September 6 having had no luck — he had not found Shannon or the lost horses. The captains made smoke at camp each evening to mark their position, and read tracks when they could, but the gap did not close. As the boat pressed on into ever emptier country, the question of what had become of Shannon hung over the men, still open.
