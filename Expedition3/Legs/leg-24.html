Decided to take a nice sunset flight this evening, on account of both the departure and arrival airports having lights, and for a nice change of pace. Took off around 8:30 PM from Sioux City. I was hoping to find the Sergeant Floyd Monument, but it appears not to be modeled in the sim. Here is a public domain image of the monument I was looking for.

Sergeant Floyd Monument - Courtesy Brian Kell / Wikipedia

There is also a Sergeant Floyd River Museum and a Lewis and Clark Interpretive Center along the river in Sioux City, but I couldn't find those either.

I did find the confluence of the Missouri and Big Sioux Rivers. This marks the boundary between Iowa, Nebraska, and South Dakota.

Nebraska off the left wing, Iowa off the right wing, and South Dakota below me

As I continued, I enjoyed the view of the sunset behind the winding river.

Sunset over the Missouri River

I chose to land in Vermillion, South Dakota so that I can follow Lewis & Clark on a side trip that they took. As Captain Claude will describe, the Native Americans told them about Spirit Mound, considered a "mountain of evil spirits" by the native people. Lewis & Clark, of course, decided this meant they must climb it as a side trip. So on my next flight, I will also take a side trip to Spirit Mound, which is just north of Vermillion.

Landed around dusk in a crosswind. Another big bounce and some work to keep it on the runway, but nothing too bad.

What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — August 21–24, 1804

A New Sergeant and a First Buffalo

The Corps pushed on from Floyd's grave under a hard southeast wind that reefed their sail and blew sand off the bars in clouds so thick they could barely see the channel ahead. About a mile and a half above Floyd's River they reached the mouth of the Big Sioux — Dorion's river, the boundary between what would become Iowa and South Dakota. The old interpreter described its course for them: navigable some seventy or eighty leagues to a pair of falls, and somewhere below the falls a creek where the natives quarried red stone for their pipes — a place of peace where no war club could be raised among any nation. (This was the catlinite quarry, in present-day southwestern Minnesota; George Catlin would visit and paint it some thirty years later, and the rock now bears his name.)

The next morning they landed at a south-bank bluff laced with pyrite, alum, copperas, and what Clark guessed was "arsenic or cobalt." Lewis, ever the natural historian, pounded the rocks to examine them — and was nearly overcome by the fumes. He prescribed himself a dose of salts that evening to purge the effects. Whatever the bluff actually held, it was almost certainly not arsenic; iron-sulfide minerals like pyrite and marcasite release sulfurous fumes when freshly broken, and a noseful at close range is exactly the kind of thing that would send a man reaching for the salts.

That same evening — three days after burying Sergeant Floyd — the captains called the men together to elect his replacement. Three names went on the slate: Patrick Gass, William Bratton, and George Gibson. Gass won handily with nineteen votes. So far as anyone has been able to tell, it was the first time United States citizens voted in an election west of the Mississippi: a small piece of democracy ordered up on a sandbar by two officers who needed a sergeant and saw no reason not to ask the men.

On the 23rd the Corps shot its first bison. Joseph Field came back from the prairie to report he had killed a "Bull Buffelow"; Lewis took ten or twelve men out to butcher it and bring the meat in. For most of the Corps it was the first bison they had ever seen — Sergeant Ordway walked the mile and a half across the grass to look at it and called the animal "as great a curiousity to me." Two elk swam the river right past the boat that day, both wounded and brought in, and the afternoon went to pickling the buffalo and jerking the venison while sand blew so thick off the bars that "the grass is white" for half a mile back from the river.

The morning of the 24th the Corps passed a blue-clay bluff that was on fire. The earth was "too hot for a man to bear his hand in" it; Ordway smelled sulfur. Clark took it for a volcano, and most of his contemporaries believed the same for nearly a century. It is now understood as the Ionia Volcano: a slowly self-igniting pyritiferous shale, the same iron-sulfide chemistry that had nearly poisoned Lewis two days earlier, only on a much larger scale. On the slumped face of the bluff Clark picked buffaloberries "double the Sise" of currants and pronounced them excellent for tarts. He went on ashore with York to hunt — a deer first, which York shouldered and packed in, then two buck elk in the evening. Clark's casual mention is the first documented evidence that York, though enslaved, carried a gun on this expedition.

That same afternoon the Corps passed the mouth of the White Stone River — the modern Vermillion — and Clark recorded what the Omaha, Sioux, and Otoe had all told them about a conical hill nine miles north of the river's mouth. It was said to be the home of "little devils" with large heads, eighteen inches tall, armed with sharp arrows that could kill at a great distance. Three Omaha men, the tradition went, had been killed by them not many years before, and no consideration would induce any of the nations to approach. Lewis and Clark, of course, resolved to go see it for themselves.
