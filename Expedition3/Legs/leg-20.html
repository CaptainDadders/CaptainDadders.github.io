A Fevered River, and a Promise in Washington

The week opened with the Missouri at its meanest. On July 5 a run of parallel sandbars and a raft of drift spun the keelboat completely around three times in a single day — twice on quicksand, once on the drift — each broach demanding the whole crew's strength to recover. The heat was punishing, and Clark blamed the river water itself, marveling that even men doing no work "will wet a Shirt in a Few minits." The country was changing as they climbed past the future site of St. Joseph: the timbered lower river was giving way to open, treeless "bald hills," and prairie so orderly Clark thought it had "the appearance of farms" — the tallgrass world this leg now crosses as the real thing.

The men paid for it. On July 7 Private Robert Frazer was "struck with the sun" and nearly became the expedition's first death; Lewis bled him and dosed him with saltpeter, the standard medicine of the day. By the 8th, five men were down with violent headaches and others with painful boils, and on the 11th Sergeant Floyd recorded flatly that "the men is all sick." Amid the misery there was at least one bright moment: stopping at a beaver lodge on the 5th, Lewis's big Newfoundland, Seaman, plunged in and drove the beavers out — one of the dog's first star turns in a journal where he becomes a real character.

The low point of discipline came on July 12, when Private Alexander Willard was court-martialed for lying down and falling asleep at his post as a sentinel. On a small party pushing into country it did not know, a sleeping guard was no minor lapse — under the Articles of War it was a capital crime, because one man's nap could let an enemy fall on the entire camp before anyone woke. The danger was no abstraction: only days earlier a fire spotted on the far shore had convinced the captains a Sioux war party was upon them, and they had loaded the keelboat's cannon and stood to arms (it proved to be their own lost men, asleep). Willard pleaded guilty to lying down but not to sleeping; convicted, he was sentenced to 100 lashes on the bare back, given 25 at a time over four evenings.

That same day, far to the east, the expedition's diplomatic side was bearing fruit. A delegation of Osage chiefs — among them the head chief White Hair, escorted from the Missouri country by Pierre Chouteau — reached Washington, where President Jefferson received them. He told them he would be "considering how we may best secure everlasting peace, friendship & commerce between the Osage nation, and the 17. United nations in whose name I speak to you, and take you by the hand." The handshake did not hold long. Within four years, William Clark himself — by then the U.S. Indian agent at St. Louis — would help negotiate the 1808 treaty under which the Osage gave up most of present-day Missouri and northern Arkansas; further cessions followed, and the nation was pushed first onto a reservation in Kansas and at last to Indian Territory in Oklahoma. The peace, friendship, and commerce proved rather less everlasting than the words that opened them.

For all the sickness and the lash, these days brim with wonder too. On the 12th Clark took a few men up the Nemaha, climbed a cluster of ancient grave-mounds rising from grass four and a half feet high, and called the view "one of the most pleasing prospects I ever beheld," reading the old earthworks as evidence the country had once been thickly settled. He carved his name and the date into a sandstone bluff beside an Indian petroglyph of animals and a boat — and as the party sailed a fast twenty miles on the 13th under a steady south wind, Lewis stopped to gather a sprig of white sage from the plain, one more entry in a catalog of plants the country was only beginning to surrender.
