Lewis and Clark spent a few days in the vicinity of present-day Pierre, SD, and so did I. They had some interesting interactions with the Lakota Sioux on their way out, whereas I just continued flying over water. The river was actually getting narrow again, almost looking like a river. And then, yet another dam. This time, the Oahe Dam, which creates Lake Oahe. This lake stretches some 231 miles, all of which used to be on the free-flowing Missouri River. It is interesting to note that after tribes were forcibly relocated to Indian Reservations near the river, over 150,000 acres of that land were lost due to the construction of the dam. Much of the flooded land was prime agricultural land. The damming of the river had a profoundly negative impact on the Native Americans living in the area. Be that as it may, I will be flying over this reservoir for a while. Quite a different experience compared to 1804.

The Severe Struggle to Get Away

The Corps of Discovery nearly didn't get out of present-day Pierre. On the afternoon of September 27, 1804 — after a second day of councils and dancing at the Lakota village at the mouth of the Bad River — Clark was returning the chiefs to the keelboat by pirogue when the steersman misjudged, crossed the keelboat's bow, and cut the anchor cable. The anchor was lost on the river bottom. At first light on the 28th, Clark and the pirogue crews dragged the river with boat hooks and weighted cords looking for it; the sand had buried it. They gave up and lashed two large stones to the bow as substitutes. Then, with the whole village watching from the bank, the captains prepared to push off. The chiefs were still aboard, intending to ride a short way upriver. As the men shoved off, the warrior-society "Soldiers" took possession of the bow cable. The Partisan — the second chief, who had been the agitator throughout the encounter — demanded a flag and tobacco as the price of release.

The captains refused. Lewis appeared at the bow and signaled the men to their stations. Clark, in the cabin with the chiefs, took the port fire from the gunner — a slow-burning fuse used to fire the swivel cannon, which was loaded with sixteen musket balls. About sixty warriors stood on the bank within a few yards, some with firearms, the rest with bows and steel-tipped arrows. Then Clark threw a single twist of tobacco to Black Buffalo, the grand chief, and (in his own words) "spoke so as to touch his pride." Black Buffalo accepted the tobacco, handed it to his soldiers, jerked the rope from them himself, and passed it to the bowman. The Corps shoved off. Lewis later summarized the morning in five words in his weather diary: "severe struggle to get away."

The Lakota presence on the bank did not end with the village. On September 29 the Partisan walked the south shore with three companions, asking to be ferried across; the captains complied. On September 30 a final band of three hundred Lakota came down a hill toward the boats; the Corps refused to land, anchored a hundred yards off, and sent ashore tobacco for their chiefs by pirogue. Later that day, with a stiff southeast breeze running waves down the river, the keelboat's stern caught on a submerged log and rocked violently enough to nearly fill her. Buffalo Medicine, the third chief, who had stayed aboard since the cable standoff, ran terrified to a corner of the boat. Once they had righted her and landed, he asked to leave. He told the captains the boat was wakȟań — sacred, supernaturally charged — and that he would go no further with them. They gave him a blanket, a knife, and some tobacco, smoked a pipe, and put him ashore. He was the last Lakota chief to leave the company.

The next day, October 1, the Corps passed the mouth of a major tributary entering from the west — four hundred yards wide, gentle in current despite its size, which Clark wrote down as "the Chien or Dog River." It was a misreading: the river is the Cheyenne, from the Lakota Šahíyena, "red (alien) talkers," for the tribe that lived along it. That evening, opposite a small French trading post a few miles above the mouth, a boy paddled across in a canoe to meet them; the trader followed shortly. He was Jean Vallé of Ste. Genevieve, Missouri, an English-speaking clerk who had wintered three hundred leagues up the Cheyenne under the Black Hills. He came aboard with goods for trade, but his real value was the intelligence he carried — a working geography of country south and southwest of them, the Black Hills retaining snow into summer, heavily timbered in pine, full of grizzly bears, pronghorn, and bighorn sheep. Today the lower thirty-five miles of the Cheyenne are no longer a river at all — they are an arm of Lake Oahe.

The Oahe Dam was authorized under the Pick-Sloan Plan of 1944, a federal water-management program that built a series of major dams along the Missouri for flood control, power generation, and irrigation. Construction at Oahe began in 1948; the river was closed in 1958, the reservoir began filling, and the project was formally dedicated by President John F. Kennedy in August 1962. The cost to the Lakota tribes whose reservations bordered the river was, by any honest accounting, severe. The reservoir flooded approximately 160,000 acres of Standing Rock and Cheyenne River reservation land combined — the most fertile and resource-rich land each tribe held. Most of what was lost was bottomland: timber, grass, gardens, wild fruits, medicinal plants, access to water, and the homes and communities of generations of tribal families. About ninety percent of the timber on both reservations was inundated. Historian Michael Lawson, in his study of the project, concluded that the Oahe Dam destroyed more Indian land than any other single public works project in American history. The waters the pilot flew over today are sitting on those acres — and also on every campsite the Corps of Discovery used between Pierre and Mobridge in late September and early October of 1804.

Above the Cheyenne, the captains began to encounter something else new: empty villages. On October 4, Lewis walked ashore onto an island in the middle of the river and examined the remains of a circular, palisaded Arikara settlement of about seventeen lodges, deserted he estimated five or six years. On October 6 they reached a larger village — eighty octagonal earth-covered lodges packed close together, abandoned only the previous spring, with bull-boats of buffalo hide still on their racks, willow mats and baskets still inside the lodges, and squashes in three varieties still growing in the garden plots. On October 7, near the mouth of the Moreau River, another wintering camp of sixty lodges, inhabited the previous winter, mats and skin canoes still intact. The Corps was moving up a corridor that had been heavily populated very recently and was now mostly empty. The reasons were not yet apparent to the captains.

George Drouillard reading grizzly bear tracks at the mouth of the Moreau River, October 7, 1804, near present-day Mobridge. Painting by Michael Haynes — www.mhaynesart.com.

One more detail from October 7 is worth recording. While walking a mile up the bank of the Moreau, Clark wrote in his field notes: "we saw the tracks of white bear which was very large." It is the grizzly's first appearance in the journals — registered as a landscape note, not a threat. The Corps camped that evening on a sand bar just above the upper point of an open prairie island Clark named Grouse Island, near present-day Mobridge.
