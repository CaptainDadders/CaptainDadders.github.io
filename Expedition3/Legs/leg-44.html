What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — Jul 30 – Aug 4, 1805

Searching for the Shoshone

By the end of July 1805, the Corps of Discovery had left the Three Forks and was working up the Jefferson River, the branch the captains believed would lead them west toward the mountains. What they needed most was the Shoshone, Sacagawea's people, who had the horses required to cross the Rockies. On July 30, Sacagawea recognized the country. She showed the captains the spot where she had been captured as a girl.

On August 1, Clark's thirty-fifth birthday, the expedition split. Lewis set out ahead on foot with three men to search for the Shoshone. He crossed a broad, level valley between snow-capped ranges, where the men feasted on ripe currants and serviceberries. The days were stifling and the nights cold enough for two blankets. He found old trails and abandoned brush lodges, but no sign of anyone recent.

Clark stayed behind with the canoes, and the work was brutal. The river was shallow, fast and full of gravel bars, so the men spent most of each day in the water dragging the boats upstream. Their feet were swollen and sore. Reubin Field killed a mountain lion seven and a half feet long. Clark found a fresh moccasin track near camp and suspected someone had been watching their fires. By August 4 the painful tumor on Clark's ankle had swollen so badly that he could no longer walk, and he rode in a canoe while the men hauled it over the stones.
