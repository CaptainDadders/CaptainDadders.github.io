It was very windy this evening when I had time to fly. Almost too windy, but I had good runway alignment for both takeoff and landing, to mostly avoid crosswinds, so I ventured out over the river-turned-lake. Almost right away, I passed what used to be the confluence of the Missouri River and the Grand River, now just another arm of the reservoir that is Lake Oahe.

Grand River on the left, Missouri River on the right

After a while, I passed my first windmills of the journey, off the right hand side. Seems to be a good spot for them, and a welcome change from the many fossil-fuel plants I had passed previously.

Windmills off the right side of the plane

Then, some more flying over open water on my way to Standing Rock Airport, on the Standing Rock Indian Reservation. Standing Rock became famous in 2016 because of the Dakota Access Pipeline. Permitting for this oil pipeline’s original route, near Bismarck, ND was denied, as it was deemed too risky for the city’s water supply. So instead, they decided to route it right past the Standing Rock Indian Reservation, and through the tribe’s main water supply. The Standing Rock Sioux Tribe staged protests and sued the US Government. The pipeline was ultimately completed over the objections of the Tribe.

Prior to that, in 1868, the Treaty of Laramie granted land, including the Black Hills to the Sioux Tribe. But then, in 1874, in direct violation of this treaty, Lieutenant Colonel George A. Custer and his cavalry entered the Black Hills, discovered gold, and started the Black Hills Gold Rush, whereupon thousands of white people illegally entered Sioux Land to remove its gold. By 1889, most of the Great Sioux Indian Reservation was taken back by the U.S. Congress, leaving the Sioux with much smaller Reservations, such as Standing Rock.

I did not know any of this as I approached Standing Rock Airport with a heavy headwind. Even though there wasn’t much of a crosswind, it made for a challenging, and almost comical landing. I saw headwind readings of 27 knots. The plane seemed to practically hover off the ground. Then, even after touching down pretty gently, the plane had so much lift that it wanted to get right back into the air, even though it was barely moving. In retrospect, with this much of a headwind, I probably should not have used full flaps. I think I simply had too much lift with this much of a headwind. Gemini says I should have even retracted the flaps to 0 on touchdown to kill lift. Well, there is still plenty to learn about landing this plane.

What Lewis & Clark were doing at this same point — Oct 8 – Oct 14, 1804

Among the Arikaras

On October 8, two miles above an island that would later be called Ashley, the Corps passed the mouth of a river the Arikaras called the We-tar-hoo — the present-day Grand River. Clark recorded it at 120 yards wide where it spread at flood, but on this October day it was choked down to twenty yards of muddy water, the banks thick with buffaloberries — “red Berries, resembling Currents,” he wrote. A mile farther came the Maropa (now Oak Creek), twenty-five yards wide and reduced almost to a trickle. And then a long, three-mile island planted with corn, beans, squashes, and tobacco came into view, with the first active Arikara village of the trip — Sawa-haini — at its center, and great numbers of people gathering on the bank to watch the keelboat pass.

The Arikaras called themselves the Sahnish. They were a Caddoan-speaking nation who, by 1804, lived in three earth-lodge villages clustered above the Grand River. A generation earlier they had numbered ten villages or more along this stretch of the Missouri; the abandoned, half-washed-out village sites the Corps had been passing since early October were the work of repeated smallpox epidemics beginning in 1780 and steady pressure from the Lakota to the south. Pierre-Antoine Tabeau — the French trader living among them as Régis Loisel’s agent, in country since 1802 — told the captains the men still able to bear arms numbered about six hundred. Clark, in his summary for the journals, put it more pointedly: “Remains of ten different tribes of Panias reduced by the Small Pox & wares with the Sioux.”

Karl Bodmer, Pachtüwa-Chtä, an Arikara Warrior, 1833–34. Public domain (Bodmer d. 1893).

The reception was warm. The Corps anchored across from Sawa-haini on the night of October 8 and held there for four days. Wet, cold wind kept the formal council from happening until October 10; in the interval, the three Arikara head men came to the boat, Tabeau joined the captains, and York — Clark’s enslaved Black servant, the first such person any of these spectators had ever seen — drew an astonished crowd. The Arikaras called him “the big medicine.” Clark noted, with audible exasperation a day later, that York “did not lose the oppertunity of his powers Strength &c.,” carrying himself as fierce, as if before the Corps had caught him he had “lived upon people.”

The council itself, on October 10, was the most successful diplomacy of the journey so far. Under an awning by the boat and beneath the American flag, Lewis read the speech he had been giving since the Otoes, and the captains created three “first chiefs” — Kakawissassa (Lighting Crow) of the lower village, Pocasse (Hay) of the second, and Piaheto (Eagle Feather) of the third. Lewis fired the air gun, the silent pneumatic rifle that had been astonishing audiences from the Ohio onward, and demonstrated a cast-iron corn mill the expedition had been hauling all the way upriver. Trade goods moved for buffalo robes. Whiskey was offered and refused: the chiefs, as Nicholas Biddle later expanded the captains’ account, said they were “surprised that their father should present to them a liquor which would make them fools.” Two Lakota men sat in on the council and watched. The Tetons, having failed to stop the boat themselves below the Bad River, were trying through back channels to keep the Arikaras from letting the Corps proceed. They failed.

On October 11 and 12 the Corps moved up to the second and third villages, was fed by each chief in turn, was given corn and beans and squashes for the boat, and watched the Grand Chief deliver the most complete Arikara speech in the journals — a speech that accepted the U.S. road-open framing in plain language (“Can you think any one Dare put their hands on your rope of your boat. No! not one dar”) and asked the captains to make peace with the Mandans on the Arikaras’ behalf. One of the three chiefs — almost certainly Piaheto — agreed to ride the keelboat north and attempt that peace himself. Music carried the boat away on the afternoon of the twelfth: Ordway noted “the fiddle playing & the horns Sounding” as the keelboat pulled out into the river and made nine and a half miles before camping above the towns.

But the same day’s Ordway entry contains a quieter line: “Newman & Reed confined.” John Newman, a private of the permanent party, had been heard uttering what a court-martial document would soon describe as “repeated expressions of a highly criminal and mutinous nature.” On October 13, halted at noon on a sandbar to convene the court, a panel of nine of his fellows heard the charge. The verdict was unanimous: seventy-five lashes on the bare back, and permanent discharge from the party engaged for North Western discovery. Newman was stripped of his arms, denied guard duty, and assigned to the red pirogue as a laboring hand — discarded from the permanent party, but still aboard. The court detained the boat two hours. They proceeded on.

The lashing was carried out the next afternoon, October 14, on a sand bar in a slow rain. The Arikara chief — Piaheto, by the strongest reading — was on board. Clark recorded the scene with the kind of unflinching honesty that runs through his journals at their best:

The punishment of this day allarmd. the Indian Chief verry much, he Cried aloud (or effected to Cry) I explained the Cause of the punishment and the necessity, He thought examples were also necessary, & he himself had made them by Death, his nation never whiped even their Children, from their burth.

The chief had executed men. He had not whipped them. Two ways of holding a community together, neither yielding to the other, set down on the same page without comment. Clark’s parenthetical — “or effected to Cry” — is itself a small honesty: he could not tell whether the tears were felt or performed, and he wrote it that way.

That evening the Corps made camp on the north bank in a cove. There was no marked line and the captains did not know they had crossed one, but by the geography of two centuries later, this was the first night of the expedition in present-day North Dakota. Lewis noted that the cottonwoods were now the only trees with leaves still on them. A cold wet wind kept blowing.
