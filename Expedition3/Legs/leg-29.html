I then reached the Big Bend of the Missouri River. When Lewis and Clark reached this feature, they estimated that the boats had to travel around 30 miles to get around the bend, while some in the party were able to walk across the neck of the bend, which they estimated at only 2000 yards. Today, the Bend survives even after the river was dammed. Today, the neck is around 1.6 miles wide, whereas the circumference of the bend is around 22 miles.

The Teton Standoff

By the time the Corps of Discovery reached the mouth of the Bad River — the stream Clark renamed the Teton, opposite present Pierre — they had entered the territory of the most powerful nation on the upper Missouri. The Teton Sioux, the western Lakota, sat astride the river's trade corridor and brokered, or blocked, the flow of goods between the St. Louis traders downstream and the Arikara, Mandan, and Hidatsa above. To the historian James Ronda, the Corps was no curiosity to them; it was a United States effort to bypass that brokerage, and the Lakota grasped as much almost at once.

The first contact, on September 24, set the pattern in miniature. A government horse, meant as a gift for a Sioux chief, was taken from John Colter while he butchered elk on an island, and the captains made its return the price of a council. The deeper problem was language: the Corps' down-river interpreters knew Omaha, not Lakota, and the men on the bank could barely follow it. Almost everything that followed passed through a thin and partial channel, much of it carried by the Omaha prisoners the Tetons themselves held.

The council convened the next day on a sandbar at the river's mouth, under a flagstaff and an awning. Three chiefs were identified — Black Buffalo, the grand chief; the Partisan, whom Clark flatly labeled a bad fellow; and Buffalo Medicine. Speeches were curtailed for want of a good interpreter, medals and a coat and a cocked hat were handed out, and the chiefs were brought aboard the keelboat to see its curiosities and given a taste of whiskey. Then the afternoon turned. On shore, young warriors seized the pirogue's cable, one hugged the mast, and the Partisan grew insolent, declaring the boats would not go on. Clark drew his sword; Lewis ordered the men under arms and the swivel guns loaded. Lakota bowmen stood with arrows drawn at point-blank range. It was Black Buffalo — the grand chief — who took hold of the rope, ordered the young men back, and chose not to press a fight he might well have won. The Corps moved a mile upstream and anchored off a willow island Clark named, with grim economy, Bad Humored Island.

What the Lakota offered the day after the near-battle was not what the rhetoric of the standoff would have predicted. On September 26 the captains were each set on a dressed white buffalo robe and carried by warriors into the village, feet never touching the ground — a great mark of friendship, as Sergeant Ordway read it. There was a pipe ceremony, a feast of dog and pemmican and prairie-turnip, and a war dance by firelight that ran past midnight. In a lodge nearby Clark counted Omaha women and boys taken captive in a recent raid, and urged the chiefs to make peace and give them up. The political world the Corps had entered was not one it had made or could undo.

The diplomacy was uneasy and the trust thin. On the night of September 27 an inexperienced steersman drove a pirogue across the keelboat's bow hard enough to snap the anchor cable, and Clark's loud orders to the oars — joined by an alarm raised ashore — brought some two hundred armed Lakota to the bank within minutes, the grand chief at their head, believing the Omahas had attacked. The misunderstanding was sorted out, but the captains read the whole sequence darkly; Cruzatte came aboard in the night to report the prisoners' warning that the Tetons meant to stop the boats. With the anchor lost, the keelboat spent the night tied to a stake beneath a caving bank, the men on full guard. The enlisted journals describe a friendly, helpful village; the captains' notes describe a band waiting for an opening. Both readings sit on the same pages, and at this point in the river neither is yet resolved.
