Pelicans, a Chief’s Grave, and the Only Death

The stretch the Corps poled and sailed across these two weeks is almost unrecognizable from the air today, and for the same reason the pilot keeps overshooting his estimates: the Missouri of 1804 was a vastly longer, looping river than the channelized one below the wing now. On August 9 the men reached a neck of land the river had recently cut through, shortening its own course by roughly fifteen miles in a single sweep; Clark walked across the three-quarter-mile isthmus on foot while the boats labored the long way around. Three days later, at what is now Snyder Bend, they spent an entire day rounding a loop that ran eighteen and three-quarter miles by water but barely half a mile across the neck by land — Clark sent a man to step it off to be sure. Those abandoned loops are the oxbow lakes — Blue Lake, Snyder Bend — that still curl beside the modern channel, fossils of the river the expedition actually traveled.

Early in the same reach the Corps passed the mouth of the Little Sioux River — the same confluence the pilot noted at the start of today’s leg. Just above it, on August 8, came one of the most extraordinary wildlife encounters of the entire journey. Lewis saw a mass of white feathers floating on the current, covering sixty or seventy yards of the river’s width and stretching downstream for three miles with no visible source — until the boats came upon an immense flock of American white pelicans packed onto a sandbar, seeming to cover several acres. Whitehouse guessed five or six thousand birds. Lewis shot one, measured it, found its pouch held five gallons of water by his reckoning, and wrote a careful natural-history description that survives in his notebook. They named the spot Pelican Island.

The pelicans are still here, which is the happy part of the story. The American white pelican never vanished from this flyway; the Missouri River corridor remains part of its migration route between Gulf Coast wintering grounds and northern-plains breeding colonies, and big flocks still gather on river sandbars and reservoir shallows through this region every spring and fall — so much so that Sioux Gateway Airport itself carries a standing caution about migratory waterfowl. The bird that astonished Lewis would not surprise a modern pilot here at all.

On August 10 the boats came within sight of Blackbird Hill — the goal the pilot went looking for today. Clark recorded the enclosed grave of Blackbird (Washinga Sahba), the late principal chief of the Omaha, atop a high round hill about three hundred feet above the prairie, bearing west about four miles. The next day, August 11, the captains and ten men climbed to the summit, raised an eight-foot pole on the burial mound, and hoisted a white flag bound with red, blue, and white — a gesture of respect, and a quiet assertion that the land had passed to the United States. From the top, Clark wrote, the Missouri could be seen meandering sixty or seventy miles.

Blackbird’s significance is hard to overstate, and it threads directly into the next several days. He had been the dominant figure on this stretch of the Missouri — a shrewd, formidable trader who controlled the river commerce and whose authority rested partly on a fearsome reputation for sorcery; he is said to have quietly poisoned rivals with arsenic obtained from white traders. He reportedly asked to be buried seated upright on his horse, on the hilltop where he had watched for traders’ boats. He died around 1800 — and he did not die alone.

What killed Blackbird also emptied the country. A smallpox epidemic swept the Omaha in the winter of 1799–1800, killing perhaps four hundred people — by Clark’s reckoning roughly half the nation. When Sergeant Ordway’s small party walked inland on August 13 to invite the Omaha to a council, carrying a white flag and tobacco, they pushed through bottomland choked with ten-foot sunflowers and thistle to reach the great village of Tonwantonga — and found it deserted and burned. The survivors were away on their summer buffalo hunt, but the village itself had been put to the torch four years earlier, in the wake of the epidemic. Clark, laid up at “Fish Camp” near present Homer, Nebraska, recorded the darkest tradition of that time: that in their grief some Omaha had burned their own town and killed their families so that all might “go together to some better country.” So the empty village the pilot’s modern map glides over was empty for two compounding reasons — the seasonal hunt, and the catastrophe that had already gutted the nation.

The Corps lingered at Fish Camp for the better part of a week, and three other threads ran through those days. The first was a search for the Sioux. On August 15, while Clark and ten men hauled a brush drag through a beaver-dammed creek and pulled out an astonishing three-hundred-odd fish (Lewis bested him the next day with a haul the men counted at over seven hundred), a column of smoke rose to the north. Lewis sent the Sioux interpreter Pierre Dorion Sr. to investigate, hoping to find a Sioux band to bring in for council. Dorion’s party returned to report the smoke was no signal at all — just trees a small Sioux party had left burning days before, fanned across the dry prairie by a hard northwest wind.

The second was a fire of their own. On August 17 the captains deliberately set the prairie alight near camp — the customary Plains signal — trying to draw in any Omaha or Sioux within sight of the smoke. None came; the Omaha were still chasing buffalo.

The third was the deserters, and here is where that thread ends. When it was left last leg, the four-man detachment — Drouillard, Reubin Field, Bratton, and Labiche — had just been sent back on August 7 under written orders to take Moses Reed “dead or alive” and to recover the engagé La Liberté. On August 17 Labiche came in ahead of the party with the news: they had caught both men, but La Liberté deceived his captors and slipped away again, and was never recovered. Reed they brought back. On August 18 — which happened to be Lewis’s thirtieth birthday — Reed was tried, confessed to deserting and stealing a public rifle and ammunition, and was sentenced to run the gauntlet four times through the party and to be expelled from the permanent corps and sent home with the return party. (Clark later struck the trial and punishment from his journal, and the episode never appeared in the published 1814 history of the expedition.) The visiting chiefs petitioned for a pardon; the captains held firm but used the moment to explain American military justice, and the day closed with an extra gill of whiskey and dancing until eleven for Lewis’s birthday.

Those visiting chiefs were not, despite some later signage, the Omaha. The leaders who arrived for the council were Oto and Missouria — “Little Thief,” an Oto, and “Big Horse,” a Missouria — come to ask the captains to broker a peace with the Omaha, who never appeared because they were still away hunting. On August 19 the captains assembled the nine Oto and Missouria leaders under an awning, delivered a formal speech, and distributed medals, commissions, and tobacco. The parley turned tense — the visitors were unhappy with their gifts and pressed repeatedly for whiskey, and at one point Clark sharply rebuked them — but it closed peaceably, with the Indians astonished by Lewis’s air gun and a magnet.

And then, on August 20, the only death. Sergeant Charles Floyd had been seized on the 19th with what the journals call “bilious colic”; he failed through the night, and the next day, after the boats put ashore to prepare a warm bath in a last hope of reviving him, he died — by Clark’s account “with a great deal of composure,” having told him, “I am going away; I want you to write me a letter.” The men carried him to the top of a high, round bluff overlooking the river and buried him with full military honors; Lewis read the funeral service, and they set a branded red cedar post at his head. They named the bluff Floyd’s Bluff and the small river just below it Floyd’s River — both names the pilot’s destination still carries today. Floyd was the first and only member of the Corps of Discovery to die on the entire journey to the Pacific and back. Modern doctors are nearly unanimous that the cause was a ruptured appendix; appendicitis would not be understood for another two decades, and no physician of 1804 could have saved him.
