A few cutoffs from the Missouri River

Took off this evening from Lonesome Sky, Missouri. The river continued to be peaceful and winding as I continued north. Eventually I reached the “Great River Platte,” as Captain Clark called it in his journal. One could follow this river to the South Platte, which goes through downtown Denver, almost 500 miles away.

Platte River off the left wing

Before long I was flying past downtown Omaha, and got a nice view of the baseball stadium that hosts the College World Series each June.

Omaha, Nebraska

Then I was approaching Eppley Field, which I believe is the largest airport that I have landed at so far. It is conveniently located right on the Missouri River, so it was a perfect place to stop. Not only that, but Lewis & Clark camped right here, on what is now airport grounds, on July 27, 1804. What was not perfect was the stiff crosswind I faced on my landing. Kept it on the pavement, though (and only a little bouncing)!

Reaching the Great Platte

The two weeks that ended where this leg lands were among the most varied of the summer. On July 14, about a mile out from camp, a black cloud unleashed a violent northeast storm that struck the keelboat near a caving, snag-lined bank and very nearly dashed her to pieces on a sand island. Every man leaped overboard to hold her off with anchor and cable while waves broke over the windward side; after some forty minutes the storm passed and the river, the men marveled, went “smooth as glass” within a minute. This was the same blow in which Clark’s field notes for the previous day had gone overboard. That same day brought a lighter moment — some of the men chased their first wild elk, a creature several of them had never before laid eyes on, with Lewis’s big Newfoundland dog Seaman joining the pursuit.

The expedition was not without its miseries. For days the men were troubled by painful boils and abscesses — “tumors,” the journals called them — and Clark, acting as the party’s surgeon, eventually lanced one swollen breast and drained it for the sufferer’s relief. Even so, Clark judged the company healthier than parties of similar size elsewhere. They had by now left behind the country of the Osage, the powerful nation whose lands had framed the early weeks of the river journey; ahead lay the Plains peoples of the Platte and beyond.

On July 21 came a milestone the boatmen treated almost as an equator: the mouth of the Great Platte River. The captains took a pirogue with six men a mile up its current, and Lewis set down one of the finest pieces of field science of the voyage — describing how the Platte’s bed of fine, light sand is “boiled up” by an impetuous torrent that builds and dissolves sandbars within hours, shoving the Missouri hard against its far bank. He corrected the common notion that the Platte colored the Missouri, noting instead that it added immense quantities of sand and quickened the current. Too shallow and too swift to navigate, the Platte was crossed by Native people in skin “bull boats.”

Ten miles above the Platte the Corps halted and built Camp White Catfish, near present Bellevue, resolving to rest several days, take astronomical observations, and attempt their first formal council with the Plains nations. They sent Drouillard and the Omaha-speaking boatman Cruzatte west with tobacco to invite the Oto and Pawnee chiefs — but the scouts returned to report the villages empty, the people all out on the plains hunting buffalo. The diplomatic timing had collided with the hunting season. While they waited, Clark bent over his maps, copying his chart of the river below to send back to President Jefferson with a returning party, and Lewis prepared papers and reports. (Curiously, no dispatch from White Catfish has ever surfaced, and it appears none was actually sent from here — the plan outran the execution.) The camp took its name from a pale catfish Goodrich caught, possibly the channel catfish, then new to science.

George Catlin, Platte River, c. 1832 — Smithsonian American Art Museum (public domain). Painted some 28 years after the expedition reached the river.

Through it all, the mosquitoes raged — some, Clark wrote, nearly “the size of house flies.” On July 27 the party loaded up, swam the last horses across for the hunters, and set sail again, examining a field of old earthen mounds where the Otoes and, before them, the Iowas had once lived. They camped that evening in a copse of cottonwood on the riverbank — at the very spot, now airport grounds, where this leg comes to rest.
