Today was a special flight for me, along a special part of the Lewis & Clark route. I reached the Three Forks today, where 3 rivers converge to officially start the Missouri River. When Lewis and Clark arrived here, they had to decide which one to ascend. It has special meaning to me because I visited this very spot, now a Montana State Park, with my brother a couple of months ago.

Three Forks. Gallatin River to the left; Missouri River below the right wing; Madison and Jefferson Rivers ahead and to the right

I decided to land atop a plateau overlooking the Missouri and Gallatin Rivers. We hiked along this very plateau during our visit, and decided it would be a cool place to land. I figured the Norden could handle the landing, and the plane was up to the task, even if I had a hard time keeping her straight on the landing.

Landing on a plateau at Missouri Headwaters State Park, Montana

After a quick stop here, I took off again and headed for the Three Forks Airport for a proper landing.

Three Rivers, No Missouri

The last week of July was a hard pull upstream. Past the crimson bluffs and the tilted limestone of the Little Gate, the current stayed fast and the men were worn thin. Lewis, who had learned to push a tolerable pole himself, worried that a river running through country this rough must be saving a serious cataract for the mountains ahead.

Clark, scouting ahead on foot with a small party, reached the Three Forks on July 25. He found the plains burned and a single horse track four or five days old, and judged all three forks about equal in size. The next day he climbed a ridge hoping for sight of Indians and saw none. On the way back he drank cold spring water while overheated and fell ill. Crossing the Jefferson, he nearly lost Charbonneau, who could not swim, to the current and risked himself to pull him out. By the time he limped into the main camp on July 27, Clark was feverish, aching, and spent.

Lewis had reached the junction that morning and climbed a limestone point to survey all three streams. The Missouri he had followed for more than a year simply stopped being one river. He could not call either the middle or southwest fork the Missouri, since they looked “as if they had been cast in the same mould.” So the captains named them instead: the Jefferson for the President, the Madison for the Secretary of State, and the Gallatin for the Secretary of the Treasury. The Jefferson, the southwest fork, was the one to follow. Lewis reasoned that it could only head among the waters of the Columbia, and that was the way west.

The Three Forks, 1868 — Alfred E. Mathews, Pencil Sketches of Montana. Painted 63 years after the expedition. Public domain.

The two rest days that followed carried a heavier weight. The island camp sat where, five years earlier, Hidatsa raiders from the Knife River had fallen on a Shoshone camp and carried off a number of women and boys, Sacagawea among them. Lewis, writing on the spot, thought she showed neither grief at the memory nor joy at being home. He had a bigger worry. There was no sign of the Shoshone anywhere, and without them and their horses he wrote that the “successfull issue of our voyage will be very doubtfull.” He was already weighing a search for them. Meanwhile the hunters kept the camp in venison, the men dressed skins into clothes and moccasins, and Clark slowly mended.
